Project summary

NoteExecutive summary

Title: Forecasting South African accommodation demand with ARIMA and SARIMA models

Section and topic: Section 1. Benchmarks, stationarity and differencing, ARMA, ARIMA and SARIMA models, the Box and Jenkins workflow, forecast evaluation.

Type of project: Implementation and model comparison on real-world data.

Question: How much forecast accuracy is gained by moving from simple benchmark methods to ARMA, ARIMA and seasonal ARIMA models for monthly stay unit nights sold in South Africa, and how sensitive is that gain to the way the COVID-19 structural break is handled?

Data: Real-world, publicly available data from Statistics South Africa (Stats SA), Tourist Accommodation statistical release P6410, series B64100 (stay unit nights sold, total industry, thousands), monthly. Analysis window January 2015 to June 2026. Full source details are in Section 2.1.

Methods: The analysis follows the ARIMA modelling procedure from the lectures (plot, transform, difference, identify, estimate, check residuals, forecast). The COVID months (March 2020 to March 2022) are treated as missing, so all models are estimated from the pre- and post-COVID data only; fitting the same models with the COVID months kept is reported as a sensitivity check. The transformation and the orders of differencing are chosen from the non-COVID data using time plots, the Guerrero estimate of the Box–Cox parameter, ACF/PACF plots and unit root tests. Candidate SARIMA models are identified by hand from the ACF and PACF, refined by an AIC search over neighbouring orders, and compared with the model chosen automatically by ARIMA(). Each candidate is checked with residual plots, Ljung–Box tests and its AR and MA roots. The candidates are then compared with four benchmarks (mean, naive, seasonal naive, drift) and simpler ARMA and ARIMA models on a 24-month test set (RMSE, MAE, MAPE, MASE, interval coverage) and by expanding-window rolling-origin cross-validation over horizons of 1 to 12 months.

Target variable: This analysis focuses on stay unit nights sold. This was chosen over income level and occupancy rate because it appears to be the most consistent. Occupancy rate can change when room supply changes anc income can be disrupted by inflation or other general increases. Unit nights sold tells a very clear demand picture with minimal factors affecting the quality of the variable over the years.

Key findings: See Section 9.1. In short: with the COVID months left in the data, the seasonal ARIMA models are no better than the seasonal naive benchmark, because the collapse in April 2020 dominates the estimated variance and distorts the parameters. Treating the COVID window as missing gives a stable SARIMA(0,1,1)(0,1,1)12 “airline” model with well behaved residuals, and that model gives the most accurate forecasts on both the test set and in cross-validation.

What I would do differently: Model the shock explicitly with intervention variables (planned for Project 3), and compare the expanding window with a sliding window that forgets the pre-COVID years.

Detailed analysis: the sections below.

1 Introduction and research question

Tourist accommodation is one of the clearest seasonal series published by Statistics South Africa. Demand peaks over the December holidays and dips in the winter months, and in April 2020 it collapsed almost to zero when the hard lockdown closed the industry. This makes it a good series for the Box and Jenkins material: the seasonality calls for seasonal differencing and a SARIMA model, while the COVID shock is a real example of a structural break that violates the assumptions those models are built on.

Forecast horizon. The forecasts are aimed at short and medium-term planning. Horizons of 1 to 3 months support operational decisions such as staffing and pricing, while horizons up to 24 months support budgeting and planning for the next two holiday seasons. 24 months is used as the longest horizon because it covers two full seasonal cycles, so every calendar month is forecast at both a short and a long lead time. Long-term forecasts of several years are not attempted: a model with both a seasonal and a non-seasonal difference has prediction intervals that widen without limit, and the COVID break shows that the structure of the series can change faster than such a horizon would allow.

Research question. How much forecast accuracy is gained by moving from benchmark methods to ARMA, ARIMA and SARIMA models for monthly stay unit nights sold, and how sensitive is this gain to the treatment of the COVID-19 break?

The project follows the five stages of a forecasting project from the notes: define the problem, gather the data, explore it, choose and fit models, and evaluate the forecasts on data the models have not seen.

After both differences (Figure 7) the series looks stationary. The ACF has a clear negative spike at lag 1 (r1 = -0.39) and a negative spike near lag 12 (r12 = 0.11), while the PACF tails off over the first few lags. Spikes at lag 1 and lag 12 in the ACF with a decaying PACF point to an MA(1) term and a seasonal MA(1) term. Following the Box and Jenkins workflow, the hand identified model is

\[ (1 - L)(1 - L^{12}) \log y_t = (1 + \theta_1 L)(1 + \Theta_1 L^{12})\,\varepsilon_t, \qquad \varepsilon_t \sim \text{WN}(0, \sigma^2), \]

the SARIMA(0,1,1)(0,1,1)12 or “airline” model. No constant is included because with d + D = 2 a constant would imply a quadratic trend in the forecasts.

4 Modelling strategy

4.1 The Box and Jenkins workflow

The modelling follows the Box and Jenkins workflow from the notes. Each step maps to a part of this report:

  1. Plot the data: the time plot, seasonal plots and STL decomposition in the exploratory analysis.
  2. Difference until approximately stationary: one seasonal and one first difference of log nights sold, chosen from the unit root tests and the ACF.
  3. Read the ACF and PACF to identify candidate orders: the lag 1 and lag 12 spikes point to the SARIMA(0,1,1)(0,1,1)12 model. The automatic search and the simpler ARMA and ARIMA models are included as alternative candidates.
  4. Estimate by maximum likelihood: ARIMA() in fable, under both treatments of the COVID months.
  5. Diagnose the residuals against the white noise standard: Ljung and Box tests, residual plots, ACF and histogram.
  6. Forecast only if the diagnostics pass; otherwise return to step 3.

Finally, a model that survives the loop must still beat the benchmarks on the test set before it earns its place. In this report, models that fail step 5 are still forecast on the test set, but only as reference points showing why the loop matters. They are not candidates for the final model.

4.2 The COVID problem

ARIMA models assume that one set of parameters describes the whole series. The COVID window breaks this in two ways. First, the April 2020 collapse produces residuals many times larger than normal, which inflates \(\hat\sigma^2\) and therefore every prediction interval. Second, the estimation tries to explain the shock and the recovery with the same MA and AR terms that describe normal month-to-month behaviour, which pulls the coefficients away from their usual values. Simply deleting the COVID months is not possible, because an ARIMA model needs equally spaced observations.

Two designs are compared:

  • Design A (COVID kept): fit every model to the observed series, including the COVID months.
  • Design B (COVID as missing): replace March 2020 to March 2022 by NA. The ARIMA likelihood is computed with the Kalman filter, which skips missing observations and simply runs the prediction step through the gap, so the parameters are estimated only from the pre-COVID and post-COVID months while the time ordering is kept intact. The benchmark methods also cope with missing values (for example, seasonal naive uses the last observed value for each month).

This is a deliberately simple treatment of the break. A more structural treatment with intervention variables is left for Project 3.

4.4 Candidate models

The differencing orders are fixed from the identification step rather than chosen by unit root tests inside ARIMA(), so that both designs use the same differencing and the comparison isolates the effect of the COVID treatment. Point forecasts from the log scale models are back-transformed with the bias adjustment that fable applies by default, so they are forecasts of the mean rather than the median.

The two designs give very different models from the same data. Under Design A the residual variance of the airline model is 0.1073 on the log scale, against 0.0008 under Design B, about 134 times larger. The coefficients move too (Table 5). Under Design A the seasonal MA coefficient is -1.000. A value at or near -1 means the seasonal MA factor \((1 + \Theta_1 L^{12})\) almost cancels the seasonal difference \((1 - L^{12})\): the model is close to non-invertible and is effectively saying that the seasonal pattern is fixed and should not have been differenced. It also sits on the boundary of the parameter space, where the usual standard errors are unreliable. Under Design B the estimate is -0.499 and the non-seasonal MA coefficient is -0.332 (against 0.280 under Design A). The Design B values are inside the invertible region and agree in sign with the negative lag 1 and lag 12 autocorrelations found in the identification step.

Under Design B the automatic search chose ARIMA(0,1,1)(0,1,2)[12] with AICc -293.3, compared with -288.0 for the hand identified model. Whether the extra flexibility is worth it is answered by the test set below, not by the AICc.

Under Design A the residual plot is dominated by a handful of enormous residuals in 2020 (the largest is 8.9 standard deviations from zero), so the normality assumption behind the prediction intervals fails badly, even though the Ljung and Box test (p = 0.912) does not detect autocorrelation. This is a useful reminder that a white noise test only checks the correlation structure: residuals can be uncorrelated and still be far from the identically distributed errors the model assumes. Under Design B the airline residuals look like white noise (p = 0.140), the ACF has no important spikes, and the histogram is roughly symmetric. The ARMA model without differencing leaves strong autocorrelation in its residuals under Design B (p = 0.000), which is expected: it has no way to represent the seasonal pattern.

Applying step 6 of the loop. Under Design B the models that pass the white noise test are: SARIMA (0,1,1)(0,1,1), SARIMA (auto). The models that fail are: ARIMA, ARMA. For a model that fails, the workflow says to return to step 3 rather than forecast. For the ARMA and non-seasonal ARIMA models, returning to step 3 means recognising from their residual ACF that the seasonal autocorrelation has not been modelled, which leads straight to the seasonal models. The automatically selected SARIMA passes as well, so it remains a candidate alongside the hand identified model. Under Design A every model technically passes the Ljung and Box test, but the airline model fails step 5 in a different way: its residuals are far from identically distributed (the COVID residuals are extreme outliers) and its seasonal MA coefficient sits on the invertibility boundary. Returning to step 3 under Design A would not fix this, because the problem is the data, not the orders. The fix is to change how the break is handled, which is what Design B does.

Candidates for the final model. Only the models that pass the loop under Design B are candidates for forecasting. The test set comparison below then checks the last condition from the notes: whether these candidates beat the benchmarks.

9 Discussion

9.1 Key findings

  1. Seasonality is the dominant and most stable feature of the series. The seasonal shape barely changed even through the COVID years, which is why the seasonal naive method is hard to beat and why the non-seasonal ARMA and ARIMA models perform poorly.
  2. How the break is handled matters more than which ARIMA model is chosen. With the COVID months kept (Design A), the seasonal ARIMA models were worse than the seasonal naive benchmark on the test set and much worse in cross-validation. The cause is a residual variance inflated by about 134 times, a seasonal MA coefficient pushed to the invertibility boundary (-1.000), and point forecasts pushed upwards by the bias adjustment.
  3. With the COVID window treated as missing (Design B), the Box and Jenkins airline model was the only model to pass the full loop and beat the benchmarks: it passed the diagnostic step and performed best, with a test RMSE of 101.6 thousand nights (MAPE 4.25%), white noise residuals and well calibrated prediction intervals. It also had the lowest cross-validated error at every horizon from 1 to 12 months.
  4. A lower AICc did not guarantee better forecasts. The automatically selected seasonal model had a lower AICc than the hand identified model but higher test RMSE, so the simpler model identified from the ACF and PACF was preferred.

9.2 Issues and limitations

  • Choice of COVID window. The window (March 2020 to March 2022) follows the national state of disaster. The data suggest the recovery continued after April 2022, so some post-COVID months still carry the recovery trend into the training data. A sensitivity check with a window ending in December 2022 would test how much this matters.
  • Treating the shock as missing discards information. Setting 25 months to missing throws away real data, and the approach would not work for a shock at the very end of a series. An intervention model (a level shift or a ramp with a gradual recovery) would use those months instead of ignoring them; this is the starting point for Project 3.
  • Level shift after COVID. Demand has recovered to slightly below its pre-COVID level. The differenced models adapt to this because they forecast from the most recent level, but the mean benchmark only did reasonably well because the recovered level happens to sit near the long-run average, which would not hold in general.
  • MASE scaling. The MASE denominator uses an in-sample period that contains the COVID collapse, so the MASE values are deflated. RMSE and MAPE were used as the main measures.
  • Bias adjustment. Back-transforming with the mean (bias adjusted) forecast is the right choice when the forecasts are added up, for example into annual totals, but it makes the forecasts very sensitive to an inflated \(\hat\sigma^2\). This was a large part of the failure of Design A and is easy to miss.
  • Short post-COVID history. Only about two years of post-COVID data are in the training set, and the test set is two years long, so the conclusions rest on a fairly short stretch of “normal” data.

9.3 What I would do differently

I would model the COVID shock directly with intervention variables in a regression with ARIMA errors, so that the 25 months are used rather than discarded, and compare a sliding window with the expanding window, since a sliding window is the natural choice when structural breaks are plausible. Project 3 takes up both ideas on the same series and the same test set, which allows a direct comparison with the SARIMA results from this project.

# Introduction and research question

Tourist accommodation is one of the clearest seasonal series published by Statistics South Africa. Demand peaks over the December holidays and dips in the winter months, and in April 2020 it collapsed almost to zero when the hard lockdown closed the industry. This makes it a good series for the Box and Jenkins material: the seasonality calls for seasonal differencing and a SARIMA model, while the COVID shock is a real example of a structural break that violates the assumptions those models are built on.

**Forecast horizon.** The forecasts are aimed at short and medium-term planning. Horizons of 1 to 3 months support operational decisions such as staffing and pricing, while horizons up to 24 months support budgeting and planning for the next two holiday seasons. 24 months is used as the longest horizon because it covers two full seasonal cycles, so every calendar month is forecast at both a short and a long lead time. Long-term forecasts of several years are not attempted: a model with both a seasonal and a non-seasonal difference has prediction intervals that widen without limit, and the COVID break shows that the structure of the series can change faster than such a horizon would allow.

**Research question.** *How much forecast accuracy is gained by moving from benchmark methods to ARMA, ARIMA and SARIMA models for monthly stay unit nights sold, and how sensitive is this gain to the treatment of the COVID-19 break?*

The project follows the five stages of a forecasting project from the notes: define the problem, gather the data, explore it, choose and fit models, and evaluate the forecasts on data the models have not seen.

$$

# Modelling strategy

## The Box and Jenkins workflow

The modelling follows the Box and Jenkins workflow from the notes. Each step maps to a part of this report:

1. **Plot the data:** the time plot, seasonal plots and STL decomposition in the exploratory analysis.
2. **Difference until approximately stationary:** one seasonal and one first difference of log nights sold, chosen from the unit root tests and the ACF.
3. **Read the ACF and PACF to identify candidate orders:** the lag 1 and lag 12 spikes point to the SARIMA(0,1,1)(0,1,1)~12~ model. The automatic search and the simpler ARMA and ARIMA models are included as alternative candidates.
4. **Estimate by maximum likelihood:** `ARIMA()` in `fable`, under both treatments of the COVID months.
5. **Diagnose the residuals against the white noise standard:** Ljung and Box tests, residual plots, ACF and histogram.
6. **Forecast only if the diagnostics pass; otherwise return to step 3.**

Finally, a model that survives the loop must still beat the benchmarks on the test set before it earns its place. In this report, models that fail step 5 are still forecast on the test set, but only as reference points showing *why* the loop matters. They are not candidates for the final model.

## The COVID problem

ARIMA models assume that one set of parameters describes the whole series. The COVID window breaks this in two ways. First, the April 2020 collapse produces residuals many times larger than normal, which inflates $\hat\sigma^2$ and therefore every prediction interval. Second, the estimation tries to explain the shock and the recovery with the same MA and AR terms that describe normal month-to-month behaviour, which pulls the coefficients away from their usual values. Simply deleting the COVID months is not possible, because an ARIMA model needs equally spaced observations.

Two designs are compared:

* **Design A (COVID kept):** fit every model to the observed series, including the COVID months.
* **Design B (COVID as missing):** replace March 2020 to March 2022 by `NA`. The ARIMA likelihood is computed with the Kalman filter, which skips missing observations and simply runs the prediction step through the gap, so the parameters are estimated only from the pre-COVID and post-COVID months while the time ordering is kept intact. The benchmark methods also cope with missing values (for example, seasonal naive uses the last observed value for each month).

This is a deliberately simple treatment of the break. A more structural treatment with intervention variables is left for Project 3.

# Rolling-origin cross-validation

A single test set is only one forecast origin, and the ranking above could change if the split moved by a few months. Following the notes, the comparison is repeated with an expanding window: the first origin is June 2023 and the origin moves forward one month at a time until June 2025, giving 25 origins. At each origin the models are refitted on all data up to that month and used to forecast 1 to 12 months ahead. The orders of the SARIMA models are kept fixed at each refit so that the exercise measures forecasting performance rather than the order selection procedure.

```{r}
#| label: cv
```

# Discussion

## Key findings {#sec-findings}

1. **Seasonality is the dominant and most stable feature of the series.** The seasonal shape barely changed even through the COVID years, which is why the seasonal naive method is hard to beat and why the non-seasonal ARMA and ARIMA models perform poorly.
2. **How the break is handled matters more than which ARIMA model is chosen.** With the COVID months kept (Design A), the seasonal ARIMA models were worse than the seasonal naive benchmark on the test set and much worse in cross-validation. The cause is a residual variance inflated by about `r fmt(get_sig2(A, "SARIMA_BJ") / get_sig2(B, "SARIMA_BJ"), 0)` times, a seasonal MA coefficient pushed to the invertibility boundary (`r fmt(sma_A, 3)`), and point forecasts pushed upwards by the bias adjustment.
3. **With the COVID window treated as missing (Design B), the Box and Jenkins airline model was the only model to pass the full loop and beat the benchmarks**: it passed the diagnostic step and performed best, with a test RMSE of `r fmt(res_get(B, "SARIMA_BJ", "RMSE"))` thousand nights (MAPE `r fmt(res_get(B, "SARIMA_BJ", "MAPE"), 2)`%), white noise residuals and well calibrated prediction intervals. It also had the lowest cross-validated error at every horizon from 1 to 12 months.
4. **A lower AICc did not guarantee better forecasts.** The automatically selected seasonal model had a `r better_or_worse(get_aicc(B, "SARIMA_auto"), get_aicc(B, "SARIMA_BJ"))` AICc than the hand identified model but `r better_or_worse(res_get(B, "SARIMA_auto", "RMSE"), res_get(B, "SARIMA_BJ", "RMSE"))` test RMSE, so the simpler model identified from the ACF and PACF was preferred.

## Issues and limitations

* **Choice of COVID window.** The window (March 2020 to March 2022) follows the national state of disaster. The data suggest the recovery continued after April 2022, so some post-COVID months still carry the recovery trend into the training data. A sensitivity check with a window ending in December 2022 would test how much this matters.
* **Treating the shock as missing discards information.** Setting 25 months to missing throws away real data, and the approach would not work for a shock at the very end of a series. An intervention model (a level shift or a ramp with a gradual recovery) would use those months instead of ignoring them; this is the starting point for Project 3.
* **Level shift after COVID.** Demand has recovered to slightly below its pre-COVID level. The differenced models adapt to this because they forecast from the most recent level, but the mean benchmark only did reasonably well because the recovered level happens to sit near the long-run average, which would not hold in general.
* **MASE scaling.** The MASE denominator uses an in-sample period that contains the COVID collapse, so the MASE values are deflated. RMSE and MAPE were used as the main measures.
* **Bias adjustment.** Back-transforming with the mean (bias adjusted) forecast is the right choice when the forecasts are added up, for example into annual totals, but it makes the forecasts very sensitive to an inflated $\hat\sigma^2$. This was a large part of the failure of Design A and is easy to miss.
* **Short post-COVID history.** Only about two years of post-COVID data are in the training set, and the test set is two years long, so the conclusions rest on a fairly short stretch of "normal" data.

## What I would do differently

I would model the COVID shock directly with intervention variables in a regression with ARIMA errors, so that the 25 months are used rather than discarded, and compare a sliding window with the expanding window, since a sliding window is the natural choice when structural breaks are plausible. Project 3 takes up both ideas on the same series and the same test set, which allows a direct comparison with the SARIMA results from this project.
