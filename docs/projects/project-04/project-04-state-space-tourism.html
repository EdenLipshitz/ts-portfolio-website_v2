Executive summary

NoteExecutive summary

Title: State space models for South African accommodation demand

Section and topic: Section 2. Linear Gaussian state space models (dynamic linear models): the Kalman filter, maximum likelihood through the prediction error decomposition, the Kalman smoother, forecasting, structural time series models and missing observations.

Type of project: Implementation from first principles on real-world data, with a simulation study and a model comparison.

Question: What do filtering, the likelihood, smoothing and prediction tell us about monthly accommodation demand in South Africa when the 25 COVID-19 months are treated as missing? How does the filter track demand through the gap, how well do the data pin down the model, how much demand was lost to COVID-19, and do the forecasts beat the Project 1 SARIMA and the Project 3 exponential smoothing models?

Data: Statistics South Africa, Tourist Accommodation release P6410, series B64100 (stay unit nights sold, thousands), monthly, January 2015 to June 2026 (Section 2).

Methods: My own Kalman filter, likelihood, Kalman smoother and forecasts, written in the notation and structure of the course notes and checked against dlm (the package used in the notes) and against the exact log likelihood of the Project 1 SARIMA. Four structural models are estimated by maximum likelihood and compared by AIC, likelihood ratio tests, profile likelihoods and residual diagnostics. A simulation study checks the lost-demand estimate, and the forecasts are evaluated on the Projects 1 and 3 test set and by rolling-origin cross-validation.

Key findings:

  • My filter reproduces the Project 1 SARIMA log likelihood exactly (147.1098) and agrees with dlm’s filter and smoother.
  • Demand behaves like a random-walk level (monthly SD about 2%) plus a slowly evolving seasonal pattern. A fixed seasonal pattern is rejected (p = 0.002), and there is almost no month-specific noise.
  • When data resumed in April 2022, demand was 31% below what the filter expected. The filter re-learned the level within a few months without being told anything about COVID-19.
  • About 22.4 million stay unit nights (48% of expected demand) were lost between March 2020 and March 2022 (95% band 19.3 to 25.5 million).
  • The best state space model (damped trend + AR(1)) has a test RMSE of 89.9 thousand nights. That beats seasonal naive (132.6) and the Project 1 SARIMA (101.6), but not the Project 3 damped Holt–Winters model (72.5).

What I would do differently: Use the exact diffuse initialisation, add the Project 3 intervention terms and an Easter regressor to the state space model, and let the variances differ before and after COVID-19.

Detailed analysis: the sections below.

1 Introduction and research questions

Projects 1 and 3 dealt with the COVID-19 collapse in three ways: deleting the months (SARIMA with missing values), modelling them (regression with intervention terms) and imputing them (so that exponential smoothing could be used). Each had a cost. The intervention model needs the shape of the shock to be specified, and imputation treats invented values as data.

A state space model offers a fourth route. Monthly demand is described by unobserved components (an underlying level of demand and a seasonal pattern) that evolve over time, and the Kalman filter updates its estimates of these components one month at a time. When a month is missing the filter simply skips the update, so the COVID months can be left out without breaking the time ordering and without inventing values. The same machinery sits behind the earlier projects: ARIMA() evaluates its likelihood with a Kalman filter, which is how the Project 1 SARIMA coped with the missing months, and every ETS model from Project 3 is a state space model. Building the filter myself, and applying every part of it to the accommodation series, is a way of opening that black box.

3 State space models for accommodation demand

3.1 The model and what each part means

Write \(y_t = \log(\text{nights sold}_t)\). The simplest model that matches Figure 1 is a local level plus seasonal model:

\[ \begin{aligned} y_t &= \mu_t + \gamma_t + \varepsilon_t, & \varepsilon_t &\sim N(0, \sigma^2_\varepsilon), \\ \mu_t &= \mu_{t-1} + \xi_t, & \xi_t &\sim N(0, \sigma^2_\xi), \\ \gamma_t &= -\textstyle\sum_{j=1}^{11} \gamma_{t-j} + \omega_t, & \omega_t &\sim N(0, \sigma^2_\omega). \end{aligned} \]

In words:

  • \(\mu_t\) is the underlying level of demand: log nights sold with the calendar effect removed. A level shock \(\xi_t\) is a permanent change in demand, such as a new air route, a visa rule or the post-COVID reset.
  • \(\gamma_t\) is the seasonal effect of the calendar month (December high, winter months low). The twelve effects sum to roughly zero over a year, and the shock \(\omega_t\) lets the pattern change slowly, for example if school holidays move or the mix of business and leisure travel changes.
  • \(\varepsilon_t\) is month-specific noise that does not persist, such as a large conference.

The three variances decide how the filter interprets a surprise: is a month with unexpectedly high sales a sign that demand has moved up for good (level), that the seasonal pattern has changed (seasonal), or a one-off (irregular)? Estimating them is the job of the likelihood.

3.2 State space form

I use the notation of the course notes (and of dlm, Petris et al., 2009). A dynamic linear model is

\[ Y_t = F \theta_t + v_t, \quad v_t \sim N(0, V), \qquad \theta_t = G \theta_{t-1} + w_t, \quad w_t \sim N(0, W), \qquad \theta_0 \sim N(m_0, C_0). \]

For the model above the state vector holds the level and the current and previous ten seasonal effects, \(\theta_t = (\mu_t, \gamma_t, \gamma_{t-1}, \dots, \gamma_{t-10})^\top\), so it has \(p = 12\) elements, and

\[ F = (1, 1, 0, \dots, 0), \quad G = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & G_\gamma \end{pmatrix}, \quad G_\gamma = \begin{pmatrix} -1 & -1 & \cdots & -1 & -1 \\ 1 & 0 & \cdots & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & \cdots & 0 & 0 \\ \vdots & & \ddots & & \vdots \\ 0 & 0 & \cdots & 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}, \quad V = \sigma^2_\varepsilon, \quad W = \text{diag}(\sigma^2_\xi, \sigma^2_\omega, 0, \dots, 0). \]

\(F\) is a row vector, as in dlm. This is the model in the notes’ Cape Town temperature example, dlmModSeas(frequency = 12) + dlmModPoly(1), except that there the seasonal states come first and the level last.

3.3 Four candidate structural models

All four models share the seasonal component above. They differ in the trend and in whether a short-run AR(1) component \(c_t\) is added:

In each case \(y_t = \mu_t + \gamma_t\, (+\, c_t) + \varepsilon_t\). The slope \(\nu_t\) is the monthly growth rate of demand. The damped trend model is the state space counterpart of the damped Holt–Winters method that did best in Project 3, and the level + seasonal model corresponds to ETS(A,N,A). The AR(1) component allows deviations from the trend that last a few months, such as a slow recovery after a shock. Each model only changes \(F\), \(G\) and \(W\); the code that filters, smooths and forecasts never changes.

3.4 Filtering, likelihood, smoothing and prediction

Filter. Following the notes, suppose that at the end of month \(t-1\) we have \(\theta_{t-1} \mid y_{1:t-1} \sim N(m_{t-1}, C_{t-1})\). Then

\[ \begin{aligned} &\text{predict the state:} & a_t &= G m_{t-1}, & R_t &= G C_{t-1} G^\top + W, \\ &\text{predict } y_t: & f_t &= F a_t, & Q_t &= F R_t F^\top + V, \\ &\text{update:} & m_t &= a_t + K_t e_t, & C_t &= R_t - K_t F R_t, \end{aligned} \]

with forecast error \(e_t = y_t - f_t\) and Kalman gain \(K_t = R_t F^\top Q_t^{-1}\). \(e_t\) is the surprise in month \(t\), \(Q_t\) its variance, and \(K_t\) says how the surprise is shared out between the level and the seasonal effects. If month \(t\) is missing (a COVID month) it carries no information, so the posterior equals the prior: \(K_t = 0\), \(m_t = a_t\) and \(C_t = R_t\). The filter carries its last view of demand forward and becomes less certain every month, because \(W\) is added to \(R_t\) in each step. In the code I compute \(C_t\) in the equivalent Joseph form \((I - K_t F) R_t (I - K_t F)^\top + K_t V K_t^\top\). It is algebraically the same as the notes’ formula, but stays a valid variance matrix when \(V\) is close to zero, which it turns out to be here.

Likelihood. As in the notes, the log likelihood is the sum of the one-step predictive densities \(y_t \mid y_{1:t-1} \sim N(f_t, Q_t)\),

\[ \ell(\psi) = -\tfrac{1}{2} \textstyle\sum_{t} \left( \log 2\pi + \log Q_t + e_t^2 / Q_t \right), \]

summed over the observed months only: the COVID months contribute nothing. The initial level and seasonal effects are unknown, so, as the notes suggest, I set \(m_0 = 0\) and \(C_0\) very large (\(10^7 I\)). I then leave the first \(d = 13\) forecast errors out of the likelihood, since they only serve to learn the starting values (the large-variance approximation to the exact diffuse filter, Durbin and Koopman, 2012, Chapter 5). The notes warn that this likelihood can be flat with several local maxima, so every model is fitted from several starting values.

Smoother. The Kalman smoother in the notes starts at \(s_n = m_n\), \(S_n = C_n\) and works backwards:

\[ s_t = m_t + J_t (s_{t+1} - a_{t+1}), \qquad S_t = C_t - J_t (R_{t+1} - S_{t+1}) J_t^\top, \qquad J_t = C_t G^\top R_{t+1}^{-1}, \]

so that \(\theta_t \mid y_{1:n} \sim N(s_t, S_t)\). Missing months need no special treatment, since there \(m_t = a_t\) and \(C_t = R_t\) already.

Two things beyond the notes. The matrices \(J_t\) give more than \(s_t\) and \(S_t\), and I need two extra results from them:

  • Covariances between months. \(\text{Cov}(\theta_s, \theta_t \mid y_{1:n}) = J_s J_{s+1} \cdots J_{t-1} S_t\) for \(s < t\). This is needed for the uncertainty of a total over several months, such as the demand lost over the COVID window. Adding up the monthly intervals would wrongly treat the months as perfectly correlated.
  • Smoothed shocks. The smoothed state shock is \(\hat w_t = s_t - G s_{t-1}\), with \(\text{Var}(w_t \mid y_{1:n}) = S_t + G S_{t-1} G^\top - S_t J_{t-1}^\top G^\top - G J_{t-1} S_t\). Since \(\text{Var}(\hat w_t) = W - \text{Var}(w_t \mid y_{1:n})\), the standardised level and seasonal shocks are auxiliary residuals: \(t\)-statistics for a break in the level or the seasonal pattern in month \(t\) (Harvey and Koopman, 1992; Durbin and Koopman, 2012, Section 2.12).

Prediction. As in the notes’ KF() example, forecasts come from running the filter on the series with \(h\) missing values attached at the end: \(f_t\) and \(Q_t\) for those months are the forecast mean and variance.

4 Implementation and checks

4.1 The filter, likelihood, smoother and forecasts

The filter follows the structure of the notes’ KF() function (predict the state, predict \(y\), update, with \(K_t = 0\) for a missing month), written for a state vector instead of a scalar.

Code 
# Kalman filter (course notes, KF()), for a state vector ---------------------
# mod = list(FF, GG, V, W, m0, C0); y may contain NA (missing months).
# a1, R1: optional first predictive distribution (used for the SARIMA check).
KF <- function(y, mod, a1 = NULL, R1 = NULL) {
  FF <- mod$FF; GG <- mod$GG; V <- mod$V; W <- mod$W
  N <- length(y); p <- ncol(FF); I_p <- diag(p)
  at <- mt <- Kt <- matrix(0, N, p); Rt <- Ct <- array(0, c(p, p, N))
  ft <- Qt <- et <- rep(NA_real_, N)
  m <- mod$m0; C <- mod$C0
  for (i in 1:N) {
    ## predict theta
    if (i == 1 && !is.null(a1)) { a <- a1; R <- R1 } else {
      a <- as.numeric(GG %*% m)
      R <- GG %*% C %*% t(GG) + W
    }
    ## predict y
    f <- sum(FF * a)
    Q <- as.numeric(FF %*% R %*% t(FF)) + V
    ## posterior: a missing month carries no information, so K = 0
    if (is.na(y[i])) {
      m <- a; C <- R
    } else {
      K <- R %*% t(FF) / Q
      e <- y[i] - f
      m <- a + as.numeric(K) * e
      IKF <- I_p - K %*% FF
      C <- IKF %*% R %*% t(IKF) + V * K %*% t(K)     # Joseph form of R - K F R
      C <- (C + t(C)) / 2
      et[i] <- e; Kt[i, ] <- K
    }
    at[i, ] <- a; Rt[, , i] <- R; ft[i] <- f; Qt[i] <- Q
    mt[i, ] <- m; Ct[, , i] <- C
  }
  list(at = at, Rt = Rt, ft = ft, Qt = Qt, et = et, Kt = Kt, mt = mt, Ct = Ct,
       m0 = mod$m0, C0 = mod$C0, N = N)
}

# Log likelihood: sum of log N(e_t; 0, Q_t) over observed months, dropping the
# first d because of the diffuse prior
KF_loglik <- function(kf, d) {
  ok <- which(!is.na(kf$et))
  if (d > 0) ok <- ok[-seq_len(d)]
  -0.5 * sum(log(2 * pi) + log(kf$Qt[ok]) + kf$et[ok]^2 / kf$Qt[ok])
}

# Standardised forecast errors e_t / sqrt(Q_t), after the diffuse period
std_resid <- function(kf, d) {
  r  <- kf$et / sqrt(kf$Qt)
  ok <- which(!is.na(r))
  r[ok[seq_len(d)]] <- NA
  r
}

# Kalman smoother (course notes): s_t = m_t + J_t (s_{t+1} - a_{t+1}) ---------
KS <- function(kf, mod) {
  GG <- mod$GG; N <- kf$N; p <- ncol(kf$mt)
  st <- matrix(0, N, p); St <- Jt <- array(0, c(p, p, N))
  st[N, ] <- kf$mt[N, ]; St[, , N] <- kf$Ct[, , N]
  for (t in (N - 1):1) {
    J <- kf$Ct[, , t] %*% t(GG) %*% solve(kf$Rt[, , t + 1])
    st[t, ] <- kf$mt[t, ] + J %*% (st[t + 1, ] - kf$at[t + 1, ])
    St[, , t] <- kf$Ct[, , t] - J %*% (kf$Rt[, , t + 1] - St[, , t + 1]) %*% t(J)
    Jt[, , t] <- J
  }
  J0 <- kf$C0 %*% t(GG) %*% solve(kf$Rt[, , 1])                 # back to theta_0
  s0 <- as.numeric(kf$m0 + J0 %*% (st[1, ] - kf$at[1, ]))
  S0 <- kf$C0 - J0 %*% (kf$Rt[, , 1] - St[, , 1]) %*% t(J0)
  list(st = st, St = St, Jt = Jt, s0 = s0, S0 = S0, J0 = J0)
}

# Covariance matrix of the smoothed signal F theta_t over consecutive months idx
# Cov(theta_s, theta_t | y) = J_s J_{s+1} ... J_{t-1} S_t for s < t
smooth_signal_cov <- function(ks, FF, idx) {
  k <- length(idx); Sig <- matrix(0, k, k)
  for (b in seq_len(k)) {
    Cst <- ks$St[, , idx[b]]
    Sig[b, b] <- FF %*% Cst %*% t(FF)
    if (b > 1) for (a in (b - 1):1) {
      Cst <- ks$Jt[, , idx[a]] %*% Cst
      Sig[a, b] <- Sig[b, a] <- FF %*% Cst %*% t(FF)
    }
  }
  Sig
}

# The same for the filter's predictions from the last observed month before idx
# Cov(theta_s, theta_t | y_{1:s-1}) = R_s (G')^(t - s) when no data arrive in between
filter_signal_cov <- function(kf, mod, idx) {
  k <- length(idx); Sig <- matrix(0, k, k)
  for (a in seq_len(k)) {
    M <- kf$Rt[, , idx[a]]
    for (b in a:k) {
      if (b > a) M <- M %*% t(mod$GG)
      Sig[a, b] <- Sig[b, a] <- mod$FF %*% M %*% t(mod$FF)
    }
  }
  Sig
}

# Smoothed state shocks w_hat_t = s_t - G s_{t-1}, standardised (auxiliary residuals)
aux_resid <- function(ks, mod) {
  GG <- mod$GG; N <- nrow(ks$st); w <- diag(mod$W); j <- which(w > 0)
  s_prev <- rbind(ks$s0, ks$st[-N, , drop = FALSE])
  w_hat  <- ks$st - s_prev %*% t(GG)
  aux <- matrix(NA_real_, N, length(j))
  for (t in 1:N) {
    S_prev <- if (t == 1) ks$S0 else ks$St[, , t - 1]
    J_prev <- if (t == 1) ks$J0 else ks$Jt[, , t - 1]
    C_lag  <- ks$St[, , t] %*% t(J_prev)                       # Cov(theta_t, theta_{t-1} | y)
    V_w    <- ks$St[, , t] + GG %*% S_prev %*% t(GG) - C_lag %*% t(GG) - GG %*% t(C_lag)
    aux[t, ] <- w_hat[t, j] / sqrt(pmax(w[j] - diag(V_w)[j], 1e-14))
  }
  list(w_hat = w_hat[, j, drop = FALSE], aux = aux, state = j)
}

# h-step forecasts: run the filter with h missing values attached (as in the notes)
KF_forecast <- function(y, mod, h) {
  kf  <- KF(c(y, rep(NA, h)), mod)
  idx <- length(y) + 1:h
  tibble(h = 1:h, mean = kf$ft[idx], var = kf$Qt[idx])
}

These functions know nothing about tourism: each candidate model is a function that turns a parameter vector into the model matrices \((F, G, V, W, m_0, C_0)\).

4.2 Building the four models and fitting them

Variances are estimated on the log scale, as in the notes’ lik.fun (dV = exp(x[1]), dW = exp(x[2])), so they stay positive.

Code 
# Parameter vector for each model (seas = FALSE fixes the seasonal variance at zero)
param_names <- function(trend, ar, seas = TRUE) {
  c("eps", "level", if (trend != "ll") "slope", if (seas) "seas",
    if (trend == "damped") "phi", if (ar) c("ar_var", "rho"))
}

unpack <- function(theta, trend, ar, seas = TRUE) {
  nm <- param_names(trend, ar, seas)
  p  <- as.list(exp(theta)); names(p) <- nm
  if (trend == "damped") p$phi <- 0.98 * plogis(theta[nm == "phi"])   # phi in (0, 0.98)
  if (ar)                p$rho <- tanh(theta[nm == "rho"])             # rho in (-1, 1)
  if (trend == "ll")     p$slope <- NA
  if (!seas)             p$seas <- 0
  p
}

# Model matrices; trend: "llt" (local linear trend), "ll" (local level), "damped"
structural_mod <- function(p, trend, ar) {
  n_tr <- if (trend == "ll") 1 else 2
  m  <- n_tr + 11 + ar
  GG <- matrix(0, m, m); FF <- matrix(0, 1, m)
  GG[1, 1] <- 1; FF[1] <- 1                                 # level
  if (n_tr == 2) {                                          # slope
    GG[1, 2] <- 1
    GG[2, 2] <- if (trend == "damped") p$phi else 1
  }
  s1 <- n_tr + 1                                            # seasonal block (11 states)
  GG[s1, s1:(s1 + 10)] <- -1
  GG[(s1 + 1):(s1 + 10), s1:(s1 + 9)] <- diag(10)
  FF[s1] <- 1
  if (ar) { GG[m, m] <- p$rho; FF[m] <- 1 }                 # AR(1) component
  w <- numeric(m)
  w[1] <- p$level; if (n_tr == 2) w[2] <- p$slope; w[s1] <- p$seas; if (ar) w[m] <- p$ar_var
  C0 <- diag(1e7, m)                                        # m0 = 0, C0 large (notes)
  if (ar) C0[m, m] <- p$ar_var / (1 - p$rho^2)              # stationary prior for c_t
  list(FF = FF, GG = GG, V = p$eps, W = diag(w, m), m0 = rep(0, m), C0 = C0, s1 = s1)
}

d_diffuse <- 13   # same number of dropped forecast errors for every model, so AIC is comparable

ssm_negll <- function(theta, y, trend, ar, seas = TRUE, d = d_diffuse) {
  -KF_loglik(KF(y, structural_mod(unpack(theta, trend, ar, seas), trend, ar)), d)
}

# Maximum likelihood from several starting values (the notes warn of flat peaks)
fit_structural <- function(y, trend, ar, seas = TRUE, start = NULL, d = d_diffuse, polish = TRUE) {
  nm  <- param_names(trend, ar, seas)
  th0 <- c(eps = log(1e-3), level = log(1e-3), slope = log(1e-5), seas = log(1e-5),

5.3 Profile likelihoods: how well do the data pin down the variances?

The individual variances control the filter’s behaviour, so it matters how precisely they are estimated. For the selected model I compute the profile log likelihood of each variance: fix it on a grid and maximise over the other two. A 95% confidence interval is the set of values whose profile is within \(\chi^2_{1, 0.95}/2 = 1.92\) of the maximum, which, unlike a Wald interval, works on the boundary. The grids reach down to \(10^{-8}\), effectively zero, so an interval can only stop at the end of a grid if the variance cannot be told apart from zero.

6.3 The steady-state gain is exponential smoothing

Code 
fit_pre <- fit_structural(log(pre_df$nights), sel$trend, sel$ar)

Once the gain has settled to a constant \(\bar K\), the update is \(m_t = a_t + \bar K e_t\): a fixed share of each surprise goes to the level and a fixed share to the current month’s seasonal effect. That is the error-correction form of additive Holt–Winters, ETS(A,N,A) (\(\ell_t = \ell_{t-1} + \alpha e_t\), \(s_t = s_{t-12} + \gamma e_t\)), with \(\alpha\) the level share and \(\gamma\) (roughly) the seasonal share.

For the selected model, the steady-state gain gives 66% of each surprise to the level and 34% to that calendar month’s seasonal effect. These add to 100% because there is no irregular component to absorb any of the surprise.

Fitted to the pre-COVID months only (ETS cannot handle the gap), the state space model’s steady-state gain is 0.41 for the level and 0.57 for the seasonal effect. ETS(A,N,A) on the same months gives \(\alpha\) = 0.24 and \(\gamma\) = 0.76. The split differs, but in both cases the two shares add to about one: a surprise in accommodation demand is almost never treated as a one-off.

The two methods split it differently partly because ETS estimates \(\alpha\) and \(\gamma\) directly, with the initial states as free parameters, while the state space model derives the gain from the variances. Partly it is the fragility of \(\gamma\) shown in Section 5.2. This is the link between Projects 3 and 4: the smoothing parameters of exponential smoothing are a steady-state Kalman gain, determined by the signal-to-noise ratios.

Because the COVID months were not used to estimate anything, the smoothed signal in the window is a model-based estimate of what demand would have been without the pandemic (Figure 9). Comparing it with what was actually sold, the model estimates that about 22.4 million stay unit nights were lost between March 2020 and March 2022 (95% band 19.3 to 25.5 million). That is 48% of the demand expected over those 25 months.

The band uses the smoothed covariances between the 25 months. Adding up the monthly intervals instead would treat the months as perfectly correlated and give a half-width of 4.4 million nights, against 3.1 million here. The months are strongly but not perfectly correlated: they share the same bridged level, but each has its own seasonal uncertainty.

Filtering and smoothing give two different counterfactuals:

  • The filter’s forecast from February 2020 asks “what did we expect before the pandemic?”. It puts the loss at 31.0 million nights (95% band 23.9 to 38.1 million) and attributes all of the drop to COVID-19.
  • The smoother asks “given where demand ended up, what path is most likely?”. Because demand after the gap was lower than before it, its counterfactual drifts down through the window, and the estimated loss is smaller. It attributes part of the drop to a lasting change in demand that, under the model, would have happened anyway.

Which is “right” depends on whether the lower post-COVID level reflects a change that was under way regardless (the smoother’s reading) or a scar left by the pandemic (closer to the filter’s). The data cannot settle this, so I report the smoother’s figure as the main estimate and the filter’s as an upper reference.

9.3 Results

Test set. The best state space model on the test set is Damped trend + AR(1) (RMSE 89.9, Table 8). For comparison, seasonal naive has 132.6, the Project 1 SARIMA 101.6 and the Project 3 damped Holt–Winters model 72.5. The lowest RMSE overall belongs to Damped HW (Project 3).

The trend specification decides the ranking. The BSM, whose almost fixed slope comes from the pre-COVID decline, has an RMSE of 156.2, worse than the seasonal naive benchmark. Letting the trend die out (damped trend, RMSE 94.0) or removing it (level + seasonal, RMSE 105.9) helps a great deal (Figure 12). The best model on the test set, Damped trend + AR(1), is not the one AIC selected (Level + seasonal, RMSE 105.9): the AIC differences were small, and one-step fit in the training data is a weak guide to which trend will extrapolate best. All four state space models under-forecast (positive bias): demand grew faster in 2024 to 2026 than the models’ level and trend imply.

The 95% intervals of the state space models cover every test month, but that says more about their width than their quality. The mean width is 627 thousand nights for the best model (545 for the SARIMA), about 7 times its RMSE. With a random-walk level and no irregular noise, all the forecast uncertainty is uncertainty about where the level will drift, and it grows steadily with the horizon. With only 24 test months, full coverage of intervals this wide is expected and not very informative; the simulation’s coverage figures are the better check that the intervals are calibrated.

Cross-validation. Over 13 origins the best model is SARIMA (Project 1) (RMSE 83.5), and the best state space model is Damped trend + AR(1) (RMSE 86.6; Table 9, Figure 13). The other state space models have BSM (113.0), Level + seasonal (92.4), Damped trend (88.4). For reference, the Project 3 damped Holt-Winters model has 89.5 and seasonal naive 112.2. Cross-validation confirms Damped trend + AR(1) as the best state space model. The airline SARIMA and the level + seasonal model have almost the same likelihood (Section 10.1), so it is not surprising that their forecasts are close.

11 Discussion

11.1 Key findings

  1. My implementation is correct on this series. It agrees with dlm’s filter and smoother on the accommodation data with the COVID months missing (largest smoothed-state difference 2.3e-08), and it reproduces the exact log likelihood of the Project 1 airline SARIMA (147.1098 against 147.1098 from arima()).
  2. Likelihood. The Level + seasonal model has the lowest AIC, but the four structural models and the airline SARIMA are within a few log likelihood units of each other. The level variance is well determined (monthly SD of underlying demand 1.7% to 2.7%), a fixed seasonal pattern is rejected (p = 0.002), the irregular variance sits on the boundary at zero, and April 2022 is by far the least likely month.
  3. Filtering. Nearly every surprise in accommodation demand is treated as lasting: the steady-state gain gives 66% to the level and 34% to the seasonal pattern, the state space version of exponential smoothing’s \(\alpha\) and \(\gamma\). Through the gap, the filter’s uncertainty about demand grew 4.3-fold. On restart, demand was 31% below the filter’s prediction, and the filter re-learned the level within a few months without being told anything about COVID.
  4. Smoothing. About 22.4 million stay unit nights (48% of expected demand; 95% band 19.3 to 25.5 million) were lost between March 2020 and March 2022, against 31.0 million if the February 2020 forecast is used as the counterfactual. Across the four models the estimate varies by 0.4 million nights. In simulations from the fitted model, the smoothed counterfactual is within about 6% of the truth in 90% of cases, and its band covers the truth in 93% of them.
  5. Prediction. On the test set the best state space model is Damped trend + AR(1) (RMSE 89.9, against 101.6 for the SARIMA and 72.5 for damped Holt–Winters), and the BSM with its fixed slope is the worst (RMSE 156.2). In cross-validation the best state space model is Damped trend + AR(1).

11.2 Issues and limitations

  • The counterfactual depends on the model. Inside the gap the smoother interpolates with a random-walk level, so the no-COVID path is close to a straight line between the pre- and post-COVID levels. The filter-versus-smoother comparison shows that the estimate of demand lost hinges on whether the lower post-COVID level is treated as something that would have happened anyway.
  • One set of variances for the whole decade. The forecast errors are more spread out after COVID than before, so the post-COVID period may need larger variances; the restart month alone has a large influence on the level variance.
  • Moving holidays and the zero irregular variance. Easter and school holidays move between March and April, and with no irregular component the model has to read any one-off month as a level or seasonal shock. The auxiliary residuals do not point clearly at the holiday months, so how much of the seasonal variance is month-specific noise remains open; an Easter regressor would settle it.
  • Approximate diffuse initialisation and boundary estimates. Using \(C_0 = 10^7 I\) and dropping 13 forecast errors uses up more than a year of data. With the irregular variance on the boundary, AIC and likelihood ratio comparisons behave non-standardly (I used the boundary-corrected test for the seasonal variance).
  • Intervals treat the variances as known. The simulation suggests this costs little for the smoothing and forecast intervals here, but it rests on the model being right.
  • Same test window throughout. All projects use one test window and a short cross-validation period, both in the post-COVID recovery, which favours models that do not extrapolate the pre-2020 decline.

11.3 What I would do differently

I would implement the exact diffuse Kalman filter instead of a large \(C_0\). I would also add the Project 3 intervention terms and an Easter regressor as regression effects inside the state space model. That way the COVID months could be used rather than skipped, and the moving holiday would no longer be read as a shock, which would also show how much of the seasonal variance is really Easter. I would let the variances differ before and after COVID.

Finally, the notes end with a Bayesian state space model in Stan. Fitting this model that way, with priors that keep the irregular variance off the boundary, would carry the parameter uncertainty into the lost-demand estimate.

# Introduction and research questions

Projects 1 and 3 dealt with the COVID-19 collapse in three ways: deleting the months (SARIMA with missing values), modelling them (regression with intervention terms) and imputing them (so that exponential smoothing could be used). Each had a cost. The intervention model needs the shape of the shock to be specified, and imputation treats invented values as data.

A state space model offers a fourth route. Monthly demand is described by unobserved components (an underlying level of demand and a seasonal pattern) that evolve over time, and the Kalman filter updates its estimates of these components one month at a time. When a month is missing the filter simply skips the update, so the COVID months can be left out without breaking the time ordering and without inventing values. The same machinery sits behind the earlier projects: `ARIMA()` evaluates its likelihood with a Kalman filter, which is how the Project 1 SARIMA coped with the missing months, and every ETS model from Project 3 is a state space model. Building the filter myself, and applying every part of it to the accommodation series, is a way of opening that black box.

The course notes list three things a state space analysis estimates (forecasting, filtering and smoothing); with the likelihood added, each answers a different question about accommodation demand:

| Operation | Uses data up to | Question about accommodation demand |
|:--|:--|:--|
| **Filtering** | the current month | What was underlying demand at each point *in real time*? How did the filter cope with 25 months without data and with the restart in April 2022? |

# State space models for accommodation demand

## The model and what each part means

Write $y_t = \log(\text{nights sold}_t)$. The simplest model that matches @fig-data is a *local level plus seasonal* model:

$$
\begin{aligned}
y_t &= \mu_t + \gamma_t + \varepsilon_t, & \varepsilon_t &\sim N(0, \sigma^2_\varepsilon), \\
\mu_t &= \mu_{t-1} + \xi_t, & \xi_t &\sim N(0, \sigma^2_\xi), \\
\gamma_t &= -\textstyle\sum_{j=1}^{11} \gamma_{t-j} + \omega_t, & \omega_t &\sim N(0, \sigma^2_\omega).
\end{aligned}
$$

In words:

* $\mu_t$ is the **underlying level of demand**: log nights sold with the calendar effect removed. A level shock $\xi_t$ is a permanent change in demand, such as a new air route, a visa rule or the post-COVID reset.
* $\gamma_t$ is the **seasonal effect** of the calendar month (December high, winter months low). The twelve effects sum to roughly zero over a year, and the shock $\omega_t$ lets the pattern change slowly, for example if school holidays move or the mix of business and leisure travel changes.
* $\varepsilon_t$ is **month-specific noise** that does not persist, such as a large conference.

The three variances decide how the filter interprets a surprise: is a month with unexpectedly high sales a sign that demand has moved up for good (level), that the seasonal pattern has changed (seasonal), or a one-off (irregular)? Estimating them is the job of the likelihood.

## State space form

I use the notation of the course notes (and of `dlm`, Petris et al., 2009). A dynamic linear model is

$$
Y_t = F \theta_t + v_t, \quad v_t \sim N(0, V), \qquad
\theta_t = G \theta_{t-1} + w_t, \quad w_t \sim N(0, W), \qquad
\theta_0 \sim N(m_0, C_0).
$$

For the model above the state vector holds the level and the current and previous ten seasonal effects, $\theta_t = (\mu_t, \gamma_t, \gamma_{t-1}, \dots, \gamma_{t-10})^\top$, so it has $p = 12$ elements, and

$$
F = (1, 1, 0, \dots, 0), \quad
G = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & G_\gamma \end{pmatrix}, \quad
G_\gamma = \begin{pmatrix} -1 & -1 & \cdots & -1 & -1 \\ 1 & 0 & \cdots & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & \cdots & 0 & 0 \\ \vdots & & \ddots & & \vdots \\ 0 & 0 & \cdots & 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}, \quad
V = \sigma^2_\varepsilon, \quad W = \text{diag}(\sigma^2_\xi, \sigma^2_\omega, 0, \dots, 0).
$$

$F$ is a row vector, as in `dlm`. This is the model in the notes' Cape Town temperature example, `dlmModSeas(frequency = 12) + dlmModPoly(1)`, except that there the seasonal states come first and the level last.

## Four candidate structural models {#sec-models}

All four models share the seasonal component above. They differ in the trend and in whether a short-run AR(1) component $c_t$ is added:

| Model | Trend | Extra component | States $p$ |
|:--|:--|:--|:--|
| **BSM** (basic structural model) | Local linear trend: $\mu_t = \mu_{t-1} + \nu_{t-1} + \xi_t$, $\nu_t = \nu_{t-1} + \zeta_t$ | none | 13 |
| **Level + seasonal** | Local level: $\mu_t = \mu_{t-1} + \xi_t$ | none | 12 |
| **Damped trend** | $\mu_t = \mu_{t-1} + \nu_{t-1} + \xi_t$, $\nu_t = \phi \nu_{t-1} + \zeta_t$, $0 < \phi < 1$ | none | 13 |
| **Damped trend + AR(1)** | as above | $c_t = \rho c_{t-1} + \kappa_t$ | 14 |

: The four structural models. All have the seasonal component of the level + seasonal model. {#tbl-models}

In each case $y_t = \mu_t + \gamma_t\, (+\, c_t) + \varepsilon_t$. The slope $\nu_t$ is the monthly growth rate of demand. The damped trend model is the state space counterpart of the damped Holt–Winters method that did best in Project 3, and the level + seasonal model corresponds to ETS(A,N,A). The AR(1) component allows deviations from the trend that last a few months, such as a slow recovery after a shock. Each model only changes $F$, $G$ and $W$; the code that filters, smooths and forecasts never changes.

## Filtering, likelihood, smoothing and prediction

**Filter.** Following the notes, suppose that at the end of month $t-1$ we have $\theta_{t-1} \mid y_{1:t-1} \sim N(m_{t-1}, C_{t-1})$. Then

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\text{predict the state:} & a_t &= G m_{t-1}, & R_t &= G C_{t-1} G^\top + W, \\
&\text{predict } y_t: & f_t &= F a_t, & Q_t &= F R_t F^\top + V, \\
&\text{update:} & m_t &= a_t + K_t e_t, & C_t &= R_t - K_t F R_t,
\end{aligned}
$$

with forecast error $e_t = y_t - f_t$ and Kalman gain $K_t = R_t F^\top Q_t^{-1}$. $e_t$ is the surprise in month $t$, $Q_t$ its variance, and $K_t$ says how the surprise is shared out between the level and the seasonal effects. **If month $t$ is missing** (a COVID month) it carries no information, so the posterior equals the prior: $K_t = 0$, $m_t = a_t$ and $C_t = R_t$. The filter carries its last view of demand forward and becomes less certain every month, because $W$ is added to $R_t$ in each step. In the code I compute $C_t$ in the equivalent *Joseph form* $(I - K_t F) R_t (I - K_t F)^\top + K_t V K_t^\top$. It is algebraically the same as the notes' formula, but stays a valid variance matrix when $V$ is close to zero, which it turns out to be here.

**Likelihood.** As in the notes, the log likelihood is the sum of the one-step predictive densities $y_t \mid y_{1:t-1} \sim N(f_t, Q_t)$,

$$
\ell(\psi) = -\tfrac{1}{2} \textstyle\sum_{t} \left( \log 2\pi + \log Q_t + e_t^2 / Q_t \right),
$$

summed over the observed months only: the COVID months contribute nothing. The initial level and seasonal effects are unknown, so, as the notes suggest, I set $m_0 = 0$ and $C_0$ very large ($10^7 I$). I then leave the first $d = 13$ forecast errors out of the likelihood, since they only serve to learn the starting values (the large-variance approximation to the exact diffuse filter, Durbin and Koopman, 2012, Chapter 5). The notes warn that this likelihood can be flat with several local maxima, so every model is fitted from several starting values.

**Smoother.** The Kalman smoother in the notes starts at $s_n = m_n$, $S_n = C_n$ and works backwards:

$$
s_t = m_t + J_t (s_{t+1} - a_{t+1}), \qquad S_t = C_t - J_t (R_{t+1} - S_{t+1}) J_t^\top, \qquad J_t = C_t G^\top R_{t+1}^{-1},
$$

so that $\theta_t \mid y_{1:n} \sim N(s_t, S_t)$. Missing months need no special treatment, since there $m_t = a_t$ and $C_t = R_t$ already.

**Two things beyond the notes.** The matrices $J_t$ give more than $s_t$ and $S_t$, and I need two extra results from them:

* **Covariances between months.** $\text{Cov}(\theta_s, \theta_t \mid y_{1:n}) = J_s J_{s+1} \cdots J_{t-1} S_t$ for $s < t$. This is needed for the uncertainty of a *total* over several months, such as the demand lost over the COVID window. Adding up the monthly intervals would wrongly treat the months as perfectly correlated.
* **Smoothed shocks.** The smoothed state shock is $\hat w_t = s_t - G s_{t-1}$, with $\text{Var}(w_t \mid y_{1:n}) = S_t + G S_{t-1} G^\top - S_t J_{t-1}^\top G^\top - G J_{t-1} S_t$. Since $\text{Var}(\hat w_t) = W - \text{Var}(w_t \mid y_{1:n})$, the standardised level and seasonal shocks are *auxiliary residuals*: $t$-statistics for a break in the level or the seasonal pattern in month $t$ (Harvey and Koopman, 1992; Durbin and Koopman, 2012, Section 2.12).

**Prediction.** As in the notes' `KF()` example, forecasts come from running the filter on the series with $h$ missing values attached at the end: $f_t$ and $Q_t$ for those months are the forecast mean and variance.

# Implementation and checks

## The filter, likelihood, smoother and forecasts

The filter follows the structure of the notes' `KF()` function (predict the state, predict $y$, update, with $K_t = 0$ for a missing month), written for a state vector instead of a scalar.

```{r}
#| label: ssm-functions

# Kalman filter (course notes, KF()), for a state vector ---------------------
# mod = list(FF, GG, V, W, m0, C0); y may contain NA (missing months).
# a1, R1: optional first predictive distribution (used for the SARIMA check).
KF <- function(y, mod, a1 = NULL, R1 = NULL) {
  FF <- mod$FF; GG <- mod$GG; V <- mod$V; W <- mod$W
  N <- length(y); p <- ncol(FF); I_p <- diag(p)
  at <- mt <- Kt <- matrix(0, N, p); Rt <- Ct <- array(0, c(p, p, N))
  ft <- Qt <- et <- rep(NA_real_, N)
  m <- mod$m0; C <- mod$C0
  for (i in 1:N) {
    ## predict theta
    if (i == 1 && !is.null(a1)) { a <- a1; R <- R1 } else {
      a <- as.numeric(GG %*% m)
      R <- GG %*% C %*% t(GG) + W
    }
    ## predict y
    f <- sum(FF * a)
    Q <- as.numeric(FF %*% R %*% t(FF)) + V
    ## posterior: a missing month carries no information, so K = 0
    if (is.na(y[i])) {
      m <- a; C <- R
    } else {
      K <- R %*% t(FF) / Q
      e <- y[i] - f
      m <- a + as.numeric(K) * e
      IKF <- I_p - K %*% FF
      C <- IKF %*% R %*% t(IKF) + V * K %*% t(K)     # Joseph form of R - K F R
      C <- (C + t(C)) / 2
      et[i] <- e; Kt[i, ] <- K
    }
    at[i, ] <- a; Rt[, , i] <- R; ft[i] <- f; Qt[i] <- Q
    mt[i, ] <- m; Ct[, , i] <- C
  }
  list(at = at, Rt = Rt, ft = ft, Qt = Qt, et = et, Kt = Kt, mt = mt, Ct = Ct,
       m0 = mod$m0, C0 = mod$C0, N = N)
}

# Log likelihood: sum of log N(e_t; 0, Q_t) over observed months, dropping the
# first d because of the diffuse prior
KF_loglik <- function(kf, d) {
  ok <- which(!is.na(kf$et))
  if (d > 0) ok <- ok[-seq_len(d)]
  -0.5 * sum(log(2 * pi) + log(kf$Qt[ok]) + kf$et[ok]^2 / kf$Qt[ok])
}

# Standardised forecast errors e_t / sqrt(Q_t), after the diffuse period
std_resid <- function(kf, d) {
  r  <- kf$et / sqrt(kf$Qt)
  ok <- which(!is.na(r))
  r[ok[seq_len(d)]] <- NA
  r
}

# Kalman smoother (course notes): s_t = m_t + J_t (s_{t+1} - a_{t+1}) ---------
KS <- function(kf, mod) {
  GG <- mod$GG; N <- kf$N; p <- ncol(kf$mt)
  st <- matrix(0, N, p); St <- Jt <- array(0, c(p, p, N))
  st[N, ] <- kf$mt[N, ]; St[, , N] <- kf$Ct[, , N]
  for (t in (N - 1):1) {
    J <- kf$Ct[, , t] %*% t(GG) %*% solve(kf$Rt[, , t + 1])
    st[t, ] <- kf$mt[t, ] + J %*% (st[t + 1, ] - kf$at[t + 1, ])
    St[, , t] <- kf$Ct[, , t] - J %*% (kf$Rt[, , t + 1] - St[, , t + 1]) %*% t(J)
    Jt[, , t] <- J
  }
  J0 <- kf$C0 %*% t(GG) %*% solve(kf$Rt[, , 1])                 # back to theta_0
  s0 <- as.numeric(kf$m0 + J0 %*% (st[1, ] - kf$at[1, ]))
  S0 <- kf$C0 - J0 %*% (kf$Rt[, , 1] - St[, , 1]) %*% t(J0)
  list(st = st, St = St, Jt = Jt, s0 = s0, S0 = S0, J0 = J0)
}

# Covariance matrix of the smoothed signal F theta_t over consecutive months idx
# Cov(theta_s, theta_t | y) = J_s J_{s+1} ... J_{t-1} S_t for s < t
smooth_signal_cov <- function(ks, FF, idx) {
  k <- length(idx); Sig <- matrix(0, k, k)
  for (b in seq_len(k)) {
    Cst <- ks$St[, , idx[b]]
    Sig[b, b] <- FF %*% Cst %*% t(FF)
    if (b > 1) for (a in (b - 1):1) {
      Cst <- ks$Jt[, , idx[a]] %*% Cst
      Sig[a, b] <- Sig[b, a] <- FF %*% Cst %*% t(FF)
    }
  }
  Sig
}

# The same for the filter's predictions from the last observed month before idx
# Cov(theta_s, theta_t | y_{1:s-1}) = R_s (G')^(t - s) when no data arrive in between
filter_signal_cov <- function(kf, mod, idx) {
  k <- length(idx); Sig <- matrix(0, k, k)
  for (a in seq_len(k)) {
    M <- kf$Rt[, , idx[a]]
    for (b in a:k) {
      if (b > a) M <- M %*% t(mod$GG)
      Sig[a, b] <- Sig[b, a] <- mod$FF %*% M %*% t(mod$FF)
    }
  }
  Sig
}

# Smoothed state shocks w_hat_t = s_t - G s_{t-1}, standardised (auxiliary residuals)
aux_resid <- function(ks, mod) {
  GG <- mod$GG; N <- nrow(ks$st); w <- diag(mod$W); j <- which(w > 0)
  s_prev <- rbind(ks$s0, ks$st[-N, , drop = FALSE])
  w_hat  <- ks$st - s_prev %*% t(GG)
  aux <- matrix(NA_real_, N, length(j))
  for (t in 1:N) {
    S_prev <- if (t == 1) ks$S0 else ks$St[, , t - 1]
    J_prev <- if (t == 1) ks$J0 else ks$Jt[, , t - 1]
    C_lag  <- ks$St[, , t] %*% t(J_prev)                       # Cov(theta_t, theta_{t-1} | y)
    V_w    <- ks$St[, , t] + GG %*% S_prev %*% t(GG) - C_lag %*% t(GG) - GG %*% t(C_lag)
    aux[t, ] <- w_hat[t, j] / sqrt(pmax(w[j] - diag(V_w)[j], 1e-14))
  }
  list(w_hat = w_hat[, j, drop = FALSE], aux = aux, state = j)
}

# h-step forecasts: run the filter with h missing values attached (as in the notes)
KF_forecast <- function(y, mod, h) {
  kf  <- KF(c(y, rep(NA, h)), mod)
  idx <- length(y) + 1:h
  tibble(h = 1:h, mean = kf$ft[idx], var = kf$Qt[idx])
}
```

These functions know nothing about tourism: each candidate model is a function that turns a parameter vector into the model matrices $(F, G, V, W, m_0, C_0)$.

## Building the four models and fitting them

Variances are estimated on the log scale, as in the notes' `lik.fun` (`dV = exp(x[1])`, `dW = exp(x[2])`), so they stay positive.

```{r}
#| label: model-builder

# Parameter vector for each model (seas = FALSE fixes the seasonal variance at zero)
param_names <- function(trend, ar, seas = TRUE) {
  c("eps", "level", if (trend != "ll") "slope", if (seas) "seas",
    if (trend == "damped") "phi", if (ar) c("ar_var", "rho"))
}

unpack <- function(theta, trend, ar, seas = TRUE) {
  nm <- param_names(trend, ar, seas)
  p  <- as.list(exp(theta)); names(p) <- nm
  if (trend == "damped") p$phi <- 0.98 * plogis(theta[nm == "phi"])   # phi in (0, 0.98)
  if (ar)                p$rho <- tanh(theta[nm == "rho"])             # rho in (-1, 1)
  if (trend == "ll")     p$slope <- NA
  if (!seas)             p$seas <- 0
  p
}

# Model matrices; trend: "llt" (local linear trend), "ll" (local level), "damped"
structural_mod <- function(p, trend, ar) {
  n_tr <- if (trend == "ll") 1 else 2
  m  <- n_tr + 11 + ar
  GG <- matrix(0, m, m); FF <- matrix(0, 1, m)
  GG[1, 1] <- 1; FF[1] <- 1                                 # level
  if (n_tr == 2) {                                          # slope
    GG[1, 2] <- 1
    GG[2, 2] <- if (trend == "damped") p$phi else 1
  }
  s1 <- n_tr + 1                                            # seasonal block (11 states)
  GG[s1, s1:(s1 + 10)] <- -1
  GG[(s1 + 1):(s1 + 10), s1:(s1 + 9)] <- diag(10)
  FF[s1] <- 1
  if (ar) { GG[m, m] <- p$rho; FF[m] <- 1 }                 # AR(1) component
  w <- numeric(m)
  w[1] <- p$level; if (n_tr == 2) w[2] <- p$slope; w[s1] <- p$seas; if (ar) w[m] <- p$ar_var
  C0 <- diag(1e7, m)                                        # m0 = 0, C0 large (notes)
  if (ar) C0[m, m] <- p$ar_var / (1 - p$rho^2)              # stationary prior for c_t
  list(FF = FF, GG = GG, V = p$eps, W = diag(w, m), m0 = rep(0, m), C0 = C0, s1 = s1)
}

d_diffuse <- 13   # same number of dropped forecast errors for every model, so AIC is comparable

ssm_negll <- function(theta, y, trend, ar, seas = TRUE, d = d_diffuse) {
  -KF_loglik(KF(y, structural_mod(unpack(theta, trend, ar, seas), trend, ar)), d)
}

# Maximum likelihood from several starting values (the notes warn of flat peaks)
fit_structural <- function(y, trend, ar, seas = TRUE, start = NULL, d = d_diffuse, polish = TRUE) {
  nm  <- param_names(trend, ar, seas)
  th0 <- c(eps = log(1e-3), level = log(1e-3), slope = log(1e-5), seas = log(1e-5),
           phi = qlogis(0.9 / 0.98), ar_var = log(1e-3), rho = atanh(0.5))[nm]
  starts <- if (is.null(start)) list(th0, th0 + 0.5, th0 - 1) else list(start)
  best <- NULL
  for (s in starts) {
    o <- optim(s, ssm_negll, y = y, trend = trend, ar = ar, seas = seas, d = d,
               method = "BFGS", control = list(maxit = 500))
    if (polish)                                   # Nelder-Mead polish; skipped in cross-validation
      o <- optim(o$par, ssm_negll, y = y, trend = trend, ar = ar, seas = seas, d = d,
                 method = "Nelder-Mead", control = list(maxit = 4000))
    if (is.null(best) || o$value < best$value) best <- o
  }
  p   <- unpack(best$par, trend, ar, seas)
  mod <- structural_mod(p, trend, ar)
  list(theta = best$par, p = p, mod = mod, kf = KF(y, mod), loglik = -best$value,
       k = length(nm), trend = trend, ar = ar, seas = seas,
       converged = best$convergence == 0)
}

## Does the seasonal pattern really change? {#sec-seasonal}

The seasonal variance is small, and Project 3 concluded that the seasonal pattern hardly changes: there, every seasonal ETS model put the seasonal smoothing parameter $\gamma$ at its lower bound. So I test it directly. I refit the selected model with the seasonal variance fixed at zero, which is the constant-seasonal model in the notes' `dlmModSeas(..., dW = 0)` example, and compare the two by a likelihood ratio test. Zero is on the boundary of the parameter space, so the null distribution of the statistic is a 50:50 mixture of a point mass at zero and $\chi^2_1$, and the $p$-value is half the usual one.

```{r}

The data reject a fixed seasonal pattern: the likelihood ratio statistic is `r fmt(lr_seas, 2)` (p = `r pfmt(p_seas)`). So the state space model needs a seasonal pattern that moves, while Project 3's ETS models did not. Refitting ETS to the pre-COVID months shows where the difference comes from:

* Without a trend, ETS(A,N,A) gives $\gamma$ = `r fmt(eg("ETS(A,N,A)", "gamma"), 2)` and $\alpha$ = `r fmt(eg("ETS(A,N,A)", "alpha"), 2)`.
* With a trend, $\gamma$ drops to `r sci(eg("ETS(A,A,A)", "gamma"))` (ETS(A,A,A)) or `r sci(eg("ETS(A,Ad,A)", "gamma"))` (ETS(A,Ad,A)).

The ETS seasonal smoothing parameter is fragile: it depends on which other component absorbs the surprises. In the state space model, the seasonal variance is almost the same in all four trend specifications (@tbl-ssm), so the evolving seasonal pattern does not depend on the trend.

What exactly the seasonal shocks capture is another matter. With the irregular variance at zero, every one-off month (a moved Easter, a big conference) has to be absorbed by either the level or the seasonal component. Part of the seasonal variance may therefore be month-specific noise that the model cannot place anywhere else. The auxiliary residuals in @sec-aux check whether such months stand out.

## Profile likelihoods: how well do the data pin down the variances? {#sec-profile}

The individual variances control the filter's behaviour, so it matters how precisely they are estimated. For the selected model I compute the *profile log likelihood* of each variance: fix it on a grid and maximise over the other two. A 95% confidence interval is the set of values whose profile is within $\chi^2_{1, 0.95}/2 = 1.92$ of the maximum, which, unlike a Wald interval, works on the boundary. The grids reach down to $10^{-8}$, effectively zero, so an interval can only stop at the end of a grid if the variance cannot be told apart from zero.

```{r}
#| label: profile
#| cache: true

nm_sel <- param_names(sel$trend, sel$ar)
profile_par <- function(which_par, grid) {
  free <- setdiff(seq_along(nm_sel), which_par)
  start <- sel$theta[free]
  sapply(grid, function(val) {
    f <- function(th) { full <- numeric(length(nm_sel)); full[free] <- th
                        full[which_par] <- log(val)
                        ssm_negll(full, y_tour, sel$trend, sel$ar) }
    o <- optim(start, f, method = "Nelder-Mead", control = list(maxit = 2000))
    start <<- o$par
    -o$value
  })
}
grids <- list(Irregular = exp(seq(log(1e-8), log(4e-4),   length.out = 30)),
              Level     = exp(seq(log(1e-4), log(1.5e-3), length.out = 30)),
              Seasonal  = exp(seq(log(1e-8), log(4e-4),   length.out = 40)))
par_index <- c(Irregular = which(nm_sel == "eps"), Level = which(nm_sel == "level"),
               Seasonal = which(nm_sel == "seas"))
prof <- bind_rows(lapply(names(grids), function(nm)
  tibble(par = nm, value = grids[[nm]], pll = profile_par(par_index[[nm]], grids[[nm]]))))
```

The three variances behave very differently (@fig-profile):

* **Level variance: well determined.** Its 95% profile interval runs `r ci_text("Level")`, so the monthly standard deviation of underlying demand is between `r pct(sqrt(pci("Level", "lo")), 1)` and `r pct(sqrt(pci("Level", "hi")), 1)`. The data are informative about how fast demand moves.
* **Seasonal variance: less well determined.** Its interval runs `r ci_text("Seasonal")`. `r if (pci("Seasonal", "lo_edge")) "The interval reaches zero even though the likelihood ratio test rejects zero; the test uses the exact fit with the variance at zero, while the grid only goes down to a small positive value." else paste0("Zero is excluded, in line with the likelihood ratio test, but the rate at which the seasonal pattern changes is known only to within a factor of about ", fmt(pci("Seasonal", "hi") / pci("Seasonal", "lo"), 0), ".")`

The data cannot distinguish "no month-specific noise" from "a little", but they rule out a large irregular component. This is why the estimate is essentially zero, why `dlm` and I find different tiny values for it (@tbl-dlm-check), and why a Wald interval based on the curvature at the maximum would be meaningless here.

## The steady-state gain is exponential smoothing

```{r}
#| label: steady-gain

fit_pre <- fit_structural(log(pre_df$nights), sel$trend, sel$ar)
K_pre   <- fit_pre$kf$Kt[nrow(pre_df), ]
```

Once the gain has settled to a constant $\bar K$, the update is $m_t = a_t + \bar K e_t$: a fixed share of each surprise goes to the level and a fixed share to the current month's seasonal effect. That is the error-correction form of additive Holt–Winters, ETS(A,N,A) ($\ell_t = \ell_{t-1} + \alpha e_t$, $s_t = s_{t-12} + \gamma e_t$), with $\alpha$ the level share and $\gamma$ (roughly) the seasonal share.

For the selected model, the steady-state gain gives **`r pct(steady_K[1])` of each surprise to the level and `r pct(steady_K[s1_sel])` to that calendar month's seasonal effect**. These add to `r pct(steady_K[1] + steady_K[s1_sel])` because there is no irregular component to absorb any of the surprise.

Fitted to the pre-COVID months only (ETS cannot handle the gap), the state space model's steady-state gain is `r fmt(K_pre[1], 2)` for the level and `r fmt(K_pre[s1_sel], 2)` for the seasonal effect. ETS(A,N,A) on the same months gives $\alpha$ = `r fmt(eg("ETS(A,N,A)", "alpha"), 2)` and $\gamma$ = `r fmt(eg("ETS(A,N,A)", "gamma"), 2)`. The split differs, but in both cases the two shares add to about one: a surprise in accommodation demand is almost never treated as a one-off.

The two methods split it differently partly because ETS estimates $\alpha$ and $\gamma$ directly, with the initial states as free parameters, while the state space model derives the gain from the variances. Partly it is the fragility of $\gamma$ shown in @sec-seasonal. This is the link between Projects 3 and 4: the smoothing parameters of exponential smoothing are a steady-state Kalman gain, determined by the signal-to-noise ratios.

```{r}

# Demand lost over the gap: total counterfactual minus total actual. The total is a
# sum of correlated log-normals, E(e^X_t) = exp(mu_t + Sig_tt / 2) and
# Cov(e^X_s, e^X_t) = E(e^X_s) E(e^X_t) (exp(Sig_st) - 1); the band is mean +- 1.96 sd.
lost_from <- function(mu, Sig, actual) {
  Ex     <- exp(mu + diag(Sig) / 2)
  total  <- sum(Ex)
  sd_tot <- sqrt(sum(outer(Ex, Ex) * (exp(Sig) - 1)))
  tibble(lost = total - sum(actual),
         lo = total - 1.96 * sd_tot - sum(actual), hi = total + 1.96 * sd_tot - sum(actual),
         share = (total - sum(actual)) / total, total = total, sd_total = sd_tot)
}

The amplitude of the seasonal pattern is stable over the decade, but the individual monthly effects drift a little, as the likelihood ratio test in @sec-seasonal requires.

Because the COVID months were not used to estimate anything, the smoothed signal in the window is a model-based estimate of what demand would have been without the pandemic (@fig-counterfactual). Comparing it with what was actually sold, the model estimates that about **`r fmt(lost_smooth$lost / 1000, 1)` million stay unit nights** were lost between March 2020 and March 2022 (95% band `r fmt(lost_smooth$lo / 1000, 1)` to `r fmt(lost_smooth$hi / 1000, 1)` million). That is `r pct(lost_smooth$share)` of the demand expected over those 25 months.

The band uses the smoothed covariances between the 25 months. Adding up the monthly intervals instead would treat the months as perfectly correlated and give a half-width of `r fmt(naive_hw / 1000, 1)` million nights, against `r fmt(1.96 * lost_smooth$sd_total / 1000, 1)` million here. The months are strongly but not perfectly correlated: they share the same bridged level, but each has its own seasonal uncertainty.

Filtering and smoothing give two different counterfactuals:

* The filter's forecast from February 2020 asks "what did we expect before the pandemic?". It puts the loss at `r fmt(lost_filter$lost / 1000, 1)` million nights (95% band `r fmt(lost_filter$lo / 1000, 1)` to `r fmt(lost_filter$hi / 1000, 1)` million) and attributes all of the drop to COVID-19.
* The smoother asks "given where demand ended up, what path is most likely?". Because demand after the gap was lower than before it, its counterfactual drifts down through the window, and the estimated loss is `r if (lost_smooth$lost < lost_filter$lost) "smaller" else "larger"`. It attributes part of the drop to a lasting change in demand that, under the model, would have happened anyway.

Which is "right" depends on whether the lower post-COVID level reflects a change that was under way regardless (the smoother's reading) or a scar left by the pandemic (closer to the filter's). The data cannot settle this, so I report the smoother's figure as the main estimate and the filter's as an upper reference.

# Simulation study: can the lost-demand estimate be trusted?

The lost-demand figure relies on three things:

* the smoother bridging a 25-month gap;
* variances estimated from `r n_lik` forecast errors;
* intervals that treat those variances as known.

To check these in this exact setting, I simulate 300 series of the same length from the fitted `r best_aic` model, starting from the smoothed state in January 2015. For each series I delete the same 25 months, re-estimate the model with my code, and compare the estimates with the simulated truth.

    as_tibble() |>

# Comparison across the portfolio

## The structural model and the Project 1 SARIMA {#sec-compare-arima}

The SARIMA check (@tbl-sarima-check) gives a bonus. The airline model's likelihood uses the same `r ll_at_arima$n` months as the structural models' (both drop 13 diffuse months from the 89 observed), so the two are on the same footing:

* The airline model has three parameters and a log likelihood of `r fmt(fit_airline$loglik, 2)` (AIC `r fmt(-2 * fit_airline$loglik + 6, 1)`).
* The `r best_aic` model, with three variances, has `r fmt(sv(best_aic, "Log lik"), 2)` (AIC `r fmt(sv(best_aic, "AIC"), 1)`).

The fits are almost identical, which is no coincidence. Once differenced by $(1-B)(1-B^{12})$, the basic structural model is an MA(13) process whose autocorrelations are close to those of the airline model (Harvey, 1989), and the level + seasonal model is the special case with no slope. Project 1's SARIMA and this project's structural model are two descriptions of nearly the same process. The structural model's advantage is that its components have a meaning.

# Discussion

## Key findings {#sec-findings}

1. **My implementation is correct on this series.** It agrees with `dlm`'s filter and smoother on the accommodation data with the COVID months missing (largest smoothed-state difference `r sci(diff_smooth)`), and it reproduces the exact log likelihood of the Project 1 airline SARIMA (`r fmt(ll_at_arima$ll, 4)` against `r fmt(fit_airline$loglik, 4)` from `arima()`).
2. **Likelihood.** The `r best_aic` model has the lowest AIC, but the four structural models and the airline SARIMA are within a few log likelihood units of each other. The level variance is well determined (monthly SD of underlying demand `r pct(sqrt(pci("Level", "lo")), 1)` to `r pct(sqrt(pci("Level", "hi")), 1)`), a fixed seasonal pattern is rejected (p = `r pfmt(p_seas)`), the irregular variance sits on the boundary at zero, and April 2022 is by far the least likely month.
3. **Filtering.** Nearly every surprise in accommodation demand is treated as lasting: the steady-state gain gives `r pct(steady_K[1])` to the level and `r pct(steady_K[s1_sel])` to the seasonal pattern, the state space version of exponential smoothing's $\alpha$ and $\gamma$. Through the gap, the filter's uncertainty about demand grew `r fmt(sd_restart / sd_feb20, 1)`-fold. On restart, demand was `r pct(1 - exp(act_restart - pred_restart))` below the filter's prediction, and the filter re-learned the level within a few months without being told anything about COVID.
4. **Smoothing.** About `r fmt(lost_smooth$lost / 1000, 1)` million stay unit nights (`r pct(lost_smooth$share)` of expected demand; 95% band `r fmt(lost_smooth$lo / 1000, 1)` to `r fmt(lost_smooth$hi / 1000, 1)` million) were lost between March 2020 and March 2022, against `r fmt(lost_filter$lost / 1000, 1)` million if the February 2020 forecast is used as the counterfactual. Across the four models the estimate varies by `r fmt((max(lost_tbl$lost) - min(lost_tbl$lost)) / 1000, 1)` million nights. In simulations from the fitted model, the smoothed counterfactual is within about `r pct(cf_spread, 0)` of the truth in 90% of cases, and its band covers the truth in `r pct(mean(sims$cf_covered), 0)` of them.
5. **Prediction.** On the test set the best state space model is `r best_ssm_test` (RMSE `r fmt(rg(best_ssm_test, "RMSE"))`, against `r fmt(rg("SARIMA (Project 1)", "RMSE"))` for the SARIMA and `r fmt(rg("Damped HW (Project 3)", "RMSE"))` for damped Holt–Winters), and the BSM with its fixed slope is the worst (RMSE `r fmt(rg("BSM", "RMSE"))`). In cross-validation the best state space model is `r cv_best_ssm`.

## Issues and limitations

* **The counterfactual depends on the model.** Inside the gap the smoother interpolates with a random-walk level, so the no-COVID path is close to a straight line between the pre- and post-COVID levels. The filter-versus-smoother comparison shows that the estimate of demand lost hinges on whether the lower post-COVID level is treated as something that would have happened anyway.
* **One set of variances for the whole decade.** The forecast errors are more spread out after COVID than before, so the post-COVID period may need larger variances; the restart month alone has a large influence on the level variance.
* **Moving holidays and the zero irregular variance.** Easter and school holidays move between March and April, and with no irregular component the model has to read any one-off month as a level or seasonal shock. The auxiliary residuals do not point clearly at the holiday months, so how much of the seasonal variance is month-specific noise remains open; an Easter regressor would settle it.
* **Approximate diffuse initialisation and boundary estimates.** Using $C_0 = 10^7 I$ and dropping 13 forecast errors uses up more than a year of data. With the irregular variance on the boundary, AIC and likelihood ratio comparisons behave non-standardly (I used the boundary-corrected test for the seasonal variance).
* **Intervals treat the variances as known.** The simulation suggests this costs little for the smoothing and forecast intervals here, but it rests on the model being right.
* **Same test window throughout.** All projects use one test window and a short cross-validation period, both in the post-COVID recovery, which favours models that do not extrapolate the pre-2020 decline.

## What I would do differently

I would implement the exact diffuse Kalman filter instead of a large $C_0$. I would also add the Project 3 intervention terms and an Easter regressor as regression effects inside the state space model. That way the COVID months could be used rather than skipped, and the moving holiday would no longer be read as a shock, which would also show how much of the seasonal variance is really Easter. I would let the variances differ before and after COVID.

Finally, the notes end with a Bayesian state space model in Stan. Fitting this model that way, with priors that keep the irregular variance off the boundary, would carry the parameter uncertainty into the lost-demand estimate.
