NoteExecutive summary

Title: State space models for South African accommodation demand

Section and topic: Section 2. Linear Gaussian state space models: the Kalman filter, maximum likelihood through the prediction error decomposition, the Kalman state and disturbance smoothers, forecasting, structural time series models and missing observations.

Type of project: Implementation from first principles on real-world data, with a simulation study and a model comparison.

Question: What do the four operations of a state space model (filtering, likelihood, smoothing and prediction) tell us about monthly accommodation demand in South Africa, when the 25 COVID-19 months are treated as missing? Specifically: how does the filter track demand in real time and through the gap, how well do the data determine the model, how much demand was lost to COVID-19, and do the forecasts beat the Project 1 SARIMA and the Project 3 exponential smoothing models?

Data: Real-world, publicly available data from Statistics South Africa, Tourist Accommodation release P6410, series B64100 (stay unit nights sold, total industry, thousands), monthly, January 2015 to June 2026. Source details are in Section 2.

Methods: I write my own Kalman filter (with missing observations), log likelihood, state and disturbance smoothers and forecasting functions, and check them on the accommodation series itself: against the base R Kalman routines, and by reproducing the exact log likelihood of the Project 1 SARIMA. Four structural models are estimated by maximum likelihood and compared by AIC, profile likelihoods and innovation diagnostics. The filter is used to follow demand month by month through the gap and the restart, the smoothers to estimate the demand lost to COVID-19 and to flag unusual months, and a simulation study from the fitted model checks whether the lost-demand estimate and its uncertainty can be trusted. Forecasts are evaluated on the same test set as Projects 1 and 3 and by rolling-origin cross-validation.

Key findings: See Section 11.1.

What I would do differently: Use the exact diffuse initialisation, add the Project 3 intervention terms and an Easter regressor to the state space model, and model the post-COVID period with its own variances.

Detailed analysis: the sections below.

1 Introduction and research questions

Projects 1 and 3 dealt with the COVID-19 collapse in three ways: deleting the months (SARIMA with missing values), modelling them (regression with intervention terms) and imputing them (so that exponential smoothing could be used). Each had a cost. The intervention model needs the shape of the shock to be specified, and imputation treats invented values as data.

A state space model offers a fourth route. Monthly demand is described by unobserved components (an underlying level of demand and a seasonal pattern) that evolve over time, and the Kalman filter updates its estimates of these components one month at a time. When a month is missing the filter simply skips the update, so the COVID months can be left out without breaking the time ordering and without inventing values. The same machinery sits behind the earlier projects: ARIMA() evaluates its likelihood with a Kalman filter, which is how the Project 1 SARIMA coped with the missing months, and every ETS model from Project 3 is a state space model in innovations form. Building the filter myself, and applying every part of it to the accommodation series, is a way of opening that black box.

A state space model does four things, and each answers a different question about accommodation demand:

3 A state space model for accommodation demand

3.1 The model and what each part means

Write \(y_t = \log(\text{nights sold}_t)\). The simplest model that matches Figure 1 is a local level plus seasonal model:

\[ \begin{aligned} y_t &= \mu_t + \gamma_t + \varepsilon_t, & \varepsilon_t &\sim N(0, \sigma^2_\varepsilon), \\ \mu_{t+1} &= \mu_t + \xi_t, & \xi_t &\sim N(0, \sigma^2_\xi), \\ \gamma_{t+1} &= -\textstyle\sum_{j=0}^{10} \gamma_{t-j} + \omega_t, & \omega_t &\sim N(0, \sigma^2_\omega). \end{aligned} \]

In words:

  • \(\mu_t\) is the underlying level of demand: log nights sold with the calendar effect removed. A level shock \(\xi_t\) is a permanent change in demand, such as a new air route, a visa rule or the post-COVID reset.
  • \(\gamma_t\) is the seasonal effect of the calendar month (December high, winter months low). The twelve effects sum to roughly zero over a year, and the shock \(\omega_t\) lets the pattern change slowly, for example if school holidays move or the mix of business and leisure travel changes.
  • \(\varepsilon_t\) is month-specific noise that does not persist, such as the timing of Easter or a large conference.

The three variances decide how the filter interprets a surprise: is a month with unexpectedly high sales a sign that demand has moved up for good (level), that the seasonal pattern has changed (seasonal), or a one-off (irregular)? Estimating them is the job of the likelihood.

3.2 State space form

Any linear Gaussian state space model can be written as (Durbin and Koopman, 2012)

\[ y_t = Z^\top \alpha_t + \varepsilon_t, \quad \varepsilon_t \sim N(0, H), \qquad \alpha_{t+1} = T \alpha_t + R \eta_t, \quad \eta_t \sim N(0, Q), \qquad \alpha_1 \sim N(a_1, P_1). \]

For the model above the state vector holds the level and the current and previous ten seasonal effects, \(\alpha_t = (\mu_t, \gamma_t, \gamma_{t-1}, \dots, \gamma_{t-10})^\top\), so \(m = 12\), and

\[ Z = (1, 1, 0, \dots, 0)^\top, \quad T = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & T_\gamma \end{pmatrix}, \quad T_\gamma = \begin{pmatrix} -1 & -1 & \cdots & -1 & -1 \\ 1 & 0 & \cdots & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & \cdots & 0 & 0 \\ \vdots & & \ddots & & \vdots \\ 0 & 0 & \cdots & 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}, \quad H = \sigma^2_\varepsilon, \quad Q = \text{diag}(\sigma^2_\xi, \sigma^2_\omega), \]

with \(R\) the \(12 \times 2\) matrix that sends \(\xi_t\) to the level and \(\omega_t\) to the current seasonal effect. The other three candidate models (Section 4.1) only change these matrices; the code that filters, smooths and forecasts never changes.

3.3 Filtering, likelihood, smoothing and prediction

Filter. Let \(a_t\) and \(P_t\) be the mean and variance of the state given the data up to month \(t-1\). For each month the filter computes

\[ v_t = y_t - Z^\top a_t, \quad F_t = Z^\top P_t Z + H, \quad K_t = T P_t Z / F_t, \quad a_{t+1} = T a_t + K_t v_t, \quad P_{t+1} = T P_t T^\top - K_t K_t^\top F_t + R Q R^\top . \]

\(v_t\) is the forecast error for month \(t\) made at the end of month \(t-1\) (the innovation), \(F_t\) its variance, and the Kalman gain \(K_t\) says how the surprise is shared out between the level and the seasonal effects. If month \(t\) is missing (a COVID month) there is nothing to learn, so \(a_{t+1} = T a_t\) and \(P_{t+1} = T P_t T^\top + R Q R^\top\): the filter carries its last view of demand forward and becomes less certain every month.

Likelihood. The innovations are independent, so the log likelihood is the prediction error decomposition

\[ \log L = -\tfrac{1}{2} \textstyle\sum_{t} \left( \log 2\pi + \log F_t + v_t^2 / F_t \right), \]

summed over the observed months only: the COVID months contribute nothing. The initial level and seasonal effects are unknown, so I use a diffuse prior, \(a_1 = 0\) and \(P_1 = \kappa I\) with \(\kappa = 10^7\), and leave the first \(d = 13\) innovations out of the likelihood, since they only serve to learn the starting values (the large-\(\kappa\) approximation to the exact diffuse filter, Durbin and Koopman, 2012, Chapter 5).

Smoothers. Working backwards from \(r_n = 0\), \(N_n = 0\) with \(L_t = T - K_t Z^\top\),

\[ r_{t-1} = Z v_t / F_t + L_t^\top r_t, \qquad N_{t-1} = Z Z^\top / F_t + L_t^\top N_t L_t, \]

(with \(r_{t-1} = T^\top r_t\) and \(N_{t-1} = T^\top N_t T\) for a missing month), the smoothed state is \(\hat\alpha_t = a_t + P_t r_{t-1}\) with variance \(V_t = P_t - P_t N_{t-1} P_t\). The disturbance smoother gives the smoothed state shocks \(\hat\eta_t = Q R^\top r_t\) with variance \(Q - Q R^\top N_t R Q\) (Durbin and Koopman, 2012, Section 4.5). Standardised, these auxiliary residuals are \(t\)-statistics for a break in the level or the seasonal pattern at month \(t\).

Prediction. Forecasting is filtering with every future month missing: the \(h\)-step forecast of \(y\) is \(Z^\top a\) with variance \(Z^\top P Z + H\), where \(a\) and \(P\) are propagated with the missing-month recursion.

# Kalman filter for a univariate linear Gaussian state space model ----------
# y may contain NA (missing months). Returns predicted states a_t, variances P_t,
# innovations v_t, their variances F_t and the gains K_t.
kfilter <- function(y, sys) {
  Z <- sys$Z; H <- sys$H; Tm <- sys$T
  RQR <- sys$R %*% sys$Q %*% t(sys$R)
  n <- length(y); m <- length(sys$a1)
  a <- matrix(0, n + 1, m); P <- array(0, c(m, m, n + 1))
  v <- Fv <- rep(NA_real_, n); K <- matrix(0, n, m)
  a[1, ] <- sys$a1; P[, , 1] <- sys$P1
  for (t in 1:n) {
    at <- a[t, ]; Pt <- P[, , t]
    if (is.na(y[t])) {                         # missing: prediction step only
      a[t + 1, ]   <- Tm %*% at
      P[, , t + 1] <- Tm %*% Pt %*% t(Tm) + RQR
    } else {                                   # observed: update and predict
      PZ <- Pt %*% Z
      Ft <- as.numeric(crossprod(Z, PZ)) + H
      vt <- y[t] - sum(Z * at)
      Kt <- (Tm %*% PZ) / Ft
      a[t + 1, ]   <- Tm %*% at + Kt * vt
      P[, , t + 1] <- Tm %*% Pt %*% t(Tm) - Kt %*% t(Kt) * Ft + RQR
      v[t] <- vt; Fv[t] <- Ft; K[t, ] <- Kt
    }
  }
  list(a = a, P = P, v = v, F = Fv, K = K, n = n)
}

# Log likelihood (prediction error decomposition), dropping the first d
# observed innovations because of the diffuse initialisation
kloglik <- function(kf, d) {
  ok <- which(!is.na(kf$v))
  ok <- ok[-seq_len(d)]
  -0.5 * sum(log(2 * pi) + log(kf$F[ok]) + kf$v[ok]^2 / kf$F[ok])
}

# Standardised innovations e_t = v_t / sqrt(F_t), after the diffuse period
std_innov <- function(kf, d) {
  e  <- kf$v / sqrt(kf$F)
  ok <- which(!is.na(e))
  e[ok[seq_len(d)]] <- NA
  e
}

# Kalman state smoother (Durbin and Koopman 2012, Section 4.4) ---------------
ksmooth <- function(kf, y, sys) {
  Z <- sys$Z; Tm <- sys$T; n <- kf$n; m <- length(sys$a1)
  r <- rep(0, m); N <- matrix(0, m, m)
  ahat <- matrix(0, n, m); V <- array(0, c(m, m, n))
  for (t in n:1) {
    if (is.na(y[t])) {
      r <- crossprod(Tm, r)
      N <- t(Tm) %*% N %*% Tm
    } else {
      L <- Tm - kf$K[t, ] %*% t(Z)
      r <- Z * kf$v[t] / kf$F[t] + crossprod(L, r)
      N <- Z %*% t(Z) / kf$F[t] + t(L) %*% N %*% L
    }
    Pt <- kf$P[, , t]
    ahat[t, ] <- kf$a[t, ] + Pt %*% r
    V[, , t]  <- Pt - Pt %*% N %*% Pt
  }
  list(ahat = ahat, V = V)
}

# Disturbance smoother for the state shocks (D&K Section 4.5) -----------------
# eta_hat_t = Q R' r_t, Var = Q - Q R' N_t R Q, where r_t, N_t come from t+1, ..., n
kdisturb <- function(kf, y, sys) {
  Z <- sys$Z; Tm <- sys$T; n <- kf$n; m <- length(sys$a1)
  RQ <- sys$R %*% sys$Q
  r <- rep(0, m); N <- matrix(0, m, m)
  eta <- eta_var <- matrix(NA_real_, n, ncol(sys$R))
  for (t in n:1) {
    eta[t, ]     <- crossprod(RQ, r)
    eta_var[t, ] <- diag(sys$Q - t(RQ) %*% N %*% RQ)
    if (is.na(y[t])) {
      r <- crossprod(Tm, r); N <- t(Tm) %*% N %*% Tm
    } else {
      L <- Tm - kf$K[t, ] %*% t(Z)
      r <- Z * kf$v[t] / kf$F[t] + crossprod(L, r)
      N <- Z %*% t(Z) / kf$F[t] + t(L) %*% N %*% L
    }
  }
  list(eta = eta, eta_var = eta_var)
}

# h-step forecasts: the filter run with all future months missing ------------
kforecast <- function(kf, sys, h) {
  a <- kf$a[kf$n + 1, ]; P <- kf$P[, , kf$n + 1]
  RQR <- sys$R %*% sys$Q %*% t(sys$R)
  mean <- var <- numeric(h)
  for (j in 1:h) {
    mean[j] <- sum(sys$Z * a)
    var[j]  <- as.numeric(t(sys$Z) %*% P %*% sys$Z) + sys$H
    a <- sys$T %*% a
    P <- sys$T %*% P %*% t(sys$T) + RQR
  }
  tibble(h = 1:h, mean = mean, var = var)
}

These five functions know nothing about tourism: each candidate model is a function that turns a parameter vector into the system matrices \((Z, H, T, R, Q, a_1, P_1)\).

4.1 Four candidate structural models

All four models share the seasonal component above. They differ in the trend and in whether a short-run AR(1) component \(c_t\) is added:

Model Trend Extra component States \(m\)
BSM (basic structural model) Local linear trend: \(\mu_{t+1} = \mu_t + \nu_t + \xi_t\), \(\nu_{t+1} = \nu_t + \zeta_t\) none 13
Level + seasonal Local level: \(\mu_{t+1} = \mu_t + \xi_t\) none 12
Damped trend \(\mu_{t+1} = \mu_t + \nu_t + \xi_t\), \(\nu_{t+1} = \phi \nu_t + \zeta_t\), \(0 < \phi < 1\) none 13
Damped trend + AR(1) as above \(c_{t+1} = \rho c_t + \kappa_t\) 14

and in each case \(y_t = \mu_t + \gamma_t\, (+\, c_t) + \varepsilon_t\). The slope \(\nu_t\) is the monthly growth rate of demand. The damped trend model is the state space counterpart of the damped Holt–Winters method that did best in Project 3, and the level + seasonal model corresponds to ETS(A,N,A). The AR(1) component allows deviations from the trend that last a few months, such as a slow recovery after a shock.

Code 
# Build the system matrices for one of the structural models ------------------
# trend: "llt" (local linear trend), "ll" (local level), "damped"
param_names <- function(trend, ar) {
  c("eps", "level", if (trend != "ll") "slope", "seas",
    if (trend == "damped") "phi", if (ar) c("ar_var", "rho"))
}

unpack <- function(theta, trend, ar) {
  nm <- param_names(trend, ar)
  p  <- as.list(exp(theta)); names(p) <- nm
  if (trend == "damped") p$phi <- 0.98 * plogis(theta[nm == "phi"])   # phi in (0, 0.98)
  if (ar)                p$rho <- tanh(theta[nm == "rho"])             # rho in (-1, 1)
  if (trend == "ll")     p$slope <- NA
  p
}

structural_sys <- function(p, trend, ar) {
  n_tr <- if (trend == "ll") 1 else 2
  m <- n_tr + 11 + ar
  Tm <- matrix(0, m, m); Z <- numeric(m)
  Tm[1, 1] <- 1; Z[1] <- 1                                  # level
  if (n_tr == 2) {                                          # slope
    Tm[1, 2] <- 1
    Tm[2, 2] <- if (trend == "damped") p$phi else 1
  }
  s1 <- n_tr + 1                                            # seasonal block (11 states)
  Tm[s1, s1:(s1 + 10)] <- -1
  Tm[(s1 + 1):(s1 + 10), s1:(s1 + 9)] <- diag(10)
  Z[s1] <- 1
  if (ar) { Tm[m, m] <- p$rho; Z[m] <- 1 }                  # AR(1) component
  shock_rows <- c(1, if (n_tr == 2) 2, s1, if (ar) m)
  shock_var  <- c(p$level, if (n_tr == 2) p$slope, p$seas, if (ar) p$ar_var)
  R <- matrix(0, m, length(shock_rows))
  R[cbind(shock_rows, seq_along(shock_rows))] <- 1
  P1 <- diag(1e7, m)                                        # diffuse prior
  if (ar) P1[m, m] <- p$ar_var / (1 - p$rho^2)              # stationary prior for c_t
  list(Z = matrix(Z), H = p$eps, T = Tm, R = R,
       Q = diag(shock_var, length(shock_var)), a1 = rep(0, m), P1 = P1)
}

d_diffuse <- 13   # same number of dropped innovations for every model, so AIC is comparable

ssm_negll <- function(theta, y, trend, ar) {
  -kloglik(kfilter(y, structural_sys(unpack(theta, trend, ar), trend, ar)), d = d_diffuse)
}

# Maximum likelihood with several starting values (the surface is flat near the boundary)
fit_structural <- function(y, trend, ar, start = NULL) {
  nm <- param_names(trend, ar)
  th0 <- c(eps = log(1e-3), level = log(1e-3), slope = log(1e-5), seas = log(1e-5),
           phi = qlogis(0.9 / 0.98), ar_var = log(1e-3), rho = atanh(0.5))[nm]
  starts <- if (is.null(start)) list(th0, th0 + 0.5, th0 - 1) else list(start)
  best <- NULL
  for (s in starts) {
    o <- optim(s, ssm_negll, y = y, trend = trend, ar = ar, method = "BFGS",
               control = list(maxit = 500))
    o <- optim(o$par, ssm_negll, y = y, trend = trend, ar = ar, method = "Nelder-Mead",
               control = list(maxit = 4000))
    if (is.null(best) || o$value < best$value) best <- o
  }
  p   <- unpack(best$par, trend, ar)
  sys <- structural_sys(p, trend, ar)
  kf  <- kfilter(y, sys)
  list(theta = best$par, p = p, sys = sys, kf = kf, loglik = -best$value,
       k = length(nm), trend = trend, ar = ar, converged = best$convergence == 0)
}

What the estimates say about accommodation demand (Table 2):

  • There is almost no month-specific noise. The irregular variance is essentially zero in every model, so the filter treats nearly every surprise as either a permanent change in demand or a change in the seasonal pattern. On the log scale, demand behaves like a random-walk level plus a slowly evolving seasonal pattern.
  • The level moves; the growth rate does not. In the level + seasonal model the level variance is 4.5e-04, a monthly standard deviation of 2.1% in underlying demand. In the BSM the slope variance is 9.8e-11, so the growth rate is effectively a fixed drift.
  • The seasonal pattern changes slowly. The seasonal variance is about a fifth of the level variance: each month’s seasonal effect drifts by roughly 0.9% a month.
  • Damping and AR(1). The damped trend model estimates \(\phi\) = 0.901, and the AR(1) component raises the log likelihood by only 0.10 for two extra parameters.
  • Model selection. The lowest AIC belongs to the Level + seasonal model, which I use for the filtering and smoothing analysis below. The AIC values span only a few units, so the data do not strongly distinguish between the trend specifications: with the growth rate barely moving, adding a slope buys almost nothing in one-step fit. Its effect shows up in long-horizon forecasts instead (Section 9).

5.3 Profile likelihoods: how well do the data pin down the variances?

The individual variances control the filter’s behaviour, so it matters how precisely they are estimated. For the selected model I compute the profile log likelihood of each variance: fix it on a grid and maximise over the other two. A 95% confidence interval is the set of values whose profile is within \(\chi^2_{1, 0.95}/2 = 1.92\) of the maximum, which, unlike a Wald interval, works on the boundary.

6.3 The steady-state gain is exponential smoothing

Once the gain has settled to a constant \(\bar K\), the filter is \(a_{t+1} = T a_t + \bar K v_t\): a fixed share of each surprise goes to the level and a fixed share to the seasonal effect. That is exactly the error-correction form of additive Holt–Winters, ETS(A,N,A), with \(\alpha\) the level share and \(\gamma\) (roughly) the seasonal share. For the selected model the steady-state gain gives 66% of each surprise to the level and 34% to that calendar month’s seasonal effect, adding to 100% because there is no irregular component to absorb any of it.

To compare like with like I fit both models to the pre-COVID months only (ETS cannot handle the gap). The state space model’s steady-state gain is 0.41 for the level and 0.57 for the seasonal effect; ETS(A,N,A) estimates \(\alpha\) = 0.24 and \(\gamma\) = 0.76. The split between level and seasonal differs, but both add to about one: both methods conclude that a surprise in accommodation demand is almost never a one-off. The two estimators differ because ETS estimates the smoothing parameters directly by least squares on one-step errors with the initial states as free parameters, while the state space model derives them from the variances, with a diffuse start. This is the link between Projects 3 and 4: the smoothing parameters of exponential smoothing are a steady-state Kalman gain, determined by the signal-to-noise ratios.

8 Simulation study: can the lost-demand estimate be trusted?

The lost-demand figure relies on the smoother bridging a 25-month gap, on variances estimated from 76 innovations, and on intervals that assume the variances are known. impute_covid <- function(df) {
  n_win <- month_num(covid_end) - month_num(covid_start) + 2
  df <- df |>
    as_tibble() |>
    mutate(t = row_number(), mon = factor(month(as.Date(Month))),
           in_win = Month >= covid_start & Month <= covid_end,

Test set. The best state space model on the test set is Damped trend + AR(1) (RMSE 89.6), against 132.6 for seasonal naive, 101.6 for the Project 1 SARIMA and 72.5 for the Project 3 damped Holt–Winters model. All four state space models under-forecast (positive bias): demand grew faster in 2024 to 2026 than the models’ level and trend imply. The 95% intervals of the state space models cover every test month, with a mean width of 629 thousand nights for the best model (545 for the SARIMA). With a random-walk level and no irregular noise, all the forecast uncertainty is uncertainty about where the level will drift, and it grows steadily with the horizon.

Cross-validation. Over 13 origins the best model is SARIMA (Project 1) (RMSE 83.5) and the best state space model is Level + seasonal (RMSE 92.4). 11 Discussion

11.1 Key findings

  1. My implementation is correct on this series. It matches the base R Kalman routines on the accommodation data with the COVID months missing (largest state difference 1.4e-07), and it reproduces the exact log likelihood of the Project 1 airline SARIMA (147.1098 against 147.1098 from arima()).
  2. Likelihood. The Level + seasonal model has the lowest AIC, but the four structural models and the airline SARIMA are within a few log likelihood units of each other. The level variance is well determined (monthly SD of underlying demand 1.7% to 2.7%), the irregular variance sits on the boundary at zero, and April 2022 is by far the least likely month.
  3. Filtering. Nearly every surprise in accommodation demand is treated as lasting: the steady-state gain gives 66% to the level and 34% to the seasonal pattern, matching the near-one smoothing parameters of exponential smoothing. Through the gap the filter’s uncertainty about demand grew 4.3-fold; on restart demand was 31% below the filter’s prediction, and the filter re-learned the level within a few months without being told anything about COVID.
  4. Smoothing. About 22.4 million stay unit nights (48% of expected demand) were lost between March 2020 and March 2022, against 31.0 million if the February 2020 forecast is used as the counterfactual. Across the four models the estimate varies by 0.4 million nights. In simulations from the fitted model the smoothed counterfactual is within about 6% of the truth in 90% of cases.
  5. Prediction. On the test set the best state space model is Damped trend + AR(1) (RMSE 89.6, against 101.6 for the SARIMA and 72.5 for damped Holt–Winters), and the BSM with its fixed slope is the worst (RMSE 156.2).

11.2 Issues and limitations

  • The counterfactual depends on the model. Inside the gap the smoother interpolates with a random-walk level, so the no-COVID path is close to a straight line between the pre- and post-COVID levels. The filter-versus-smoother comparison shows that the estimate of demand lost hinges on whether the lower post-COVID level is treated as something that would have happened anyway.
  • One set of variances for the whole decade. The innovations are more spread out after COVID than before, so the post-COVID period may need larger variances; the restart month alone has a large influence on the level variance.
  • Moving holidays. Easter and school holidays move between March and April, and the model reads this as level or seasonal shocks.
  • Approximate diffuse initialisation and boundary estimates. Using \(\kappa = 10^7\) and dropping 13 innovations uses up more than a year of data, and with the irregular variance on the boundary, AIC and likelihood ratio comparisons behave non-standardly.
  • Intervals ignore parameter uncertainty. The smoothing and forecast intervals treat the variances as known; the simulation study shows how much this matters for the total lost demand.
  • Same test window throughout. All projects use one test window and a short cross-validation period, both in the post-COVID recovery, which favours models that do not extrapolate the pre-2020 decline.

11.3 What I would do differently

I would implement the exact diffuse Kalman filter, add the Project 3 intervention terms and an Easter regressor as regression effects inside the state space model (so the COVID months could be used rather than skipped and the moving holiday would no longer be read as a shock), and let the variances differ before and after COVID. A Bayesian treatment of the variances, with priors that keep the irregular variance off the boundary, would carry the parameter uncertainty into the lost-demand estimate, and a simulation smoother would give a proper interval for the total demand lost.

# Introduction and research questions

Projects 1 and 3 dealt with the COVID-19 collapse in three ways: deleting the months (SARIMA with missing values), modelling them (regression with intervention terms) and imputing them (so that exponential smoothing could be used). Each had a cost. The intervention model needs the shape of the shock to be specified, and imputation treats invented values as data.

A state space model offers a fourth route. Monthly demand is described by unobserved components (an underlying level of demand and a seasonal pattern) that evolve over time, and the Kalman filter updates its estimates of these components one month at a time. When a month is missing the filter simply skips the update, so the COVID months can be left out without breaking the time ordering and without inventing values. The same machinery sits behind the earlier projects: `ARIMA()` evaluates its likelihood with a Kalman filter, which is how the Project 1 SARIMA coped with the missing months, and every ETS model from Project 3 is a state space model in innovations form. Building the filter myself, and applying every part of it to the accommodation series, is a way of opening that black box.

A state space model does four things, and each answers a different question about accommodation demand:

| Operation | Uses data up to | Question about accommodation demand |
|:--|:--|:--|
| **Filtering** | the current month | What was underlying demand at each point *in real time*? How did the filter cope with 25 months without data and with the restart in April 2022? |

# A state space model for accommodation demand

## The model and what each part means

Write $y_t = \log(\text{nights sold}_t)$. The simplest model that matches @fig-data is a *local level plus seasonal* model:

$$
\begin{aligned}
y_t &= \mu_t + \gamma_t + \varepsilon_t, & \varepsilon_t &\sim N(0, \sigma^2_\varepsilon), \\
\mu_{t+1} &= \mu_t + \xi_t, & \xi_t &\sim N(0, \sigma^2_\xi), \\
\gamma_{t+1} &= -\textstyle\sum_{j=0}^{10} \gamma_{t-j} + \omega_t, & \omega_t &\sim N(0, \sigma^2_\omega).
\end{aligned}
$$

In words:

* $\mu_t$ is the **underlying level of demand**: log nights sold with the calendar effect removed. A level shock $\xi_t$ is a permanent change in demand, such as a new air route, a visa rule or the post-COVID reset.
* $\gamma_t$ is the **seasonal effect** of the calendar month (December high, winter months low). The twelve effects sum to roughly zero over a year, and the shock $\omega_t$ lets the pattern change slowly, for example if school holidays move or the mix of business and leisure travel changes.
* $\varepsilon_t$ is **month-specific noise** that does not persist, such as the timing of Easter or a large conference.

The three variances decide how the filter interprets a surprise: is a month with unexpectedly high sales a sign that demand has moved up for good (level), that the seasonal pattern has changed (seasonal), or a one-off (irregular)? Estimating them is the job of the likelihood.

## State space form

Any linear Gaussian state space model can be written as (Durbin and Koopman, 2012)

$$
y_t = Z^\top \alpha_t + \varepsilon_t, \quad \varepsilon_t \sim N(0, H), \qquad
\alpha_{t+1} = T \alpha_t + R \eta_t, \quad \eta_t \sim N(0, Q), \qquad \alpha_1 \sim N(a_1, P_1).
$$

For the model above the state vector holds the level and the current and previous ten seasonal effects, $\alpha_t = (\mu_t, \gamma_t, \gamma_{t-1}, \dots, \gamma_{t-10})^\top$, so $m = 12$, and

$$
Z = (1, 1, 0, \dots, 0)^\top, \quad
T = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & T_\gamma \end{pmatrix}, \quad
T_\gamma = \begin{pmatrix} -1 & -1 & \cdots & -1 & -1 \\ 1 & 0 & \cdots & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & \cdots & 0 & 0 \\ \vdots & & \ddots & & \vdots \\ 0 & 0 & \cdots & 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}, \quad
H = \sigma^2_\varepsilon, \quad Q = \text{diag}(\sigma^2_\xi, \sigma^2_\omega),
$$

with $R$ the $12 \times 2$ matrix that sends $\xi_t$ to the level and $\omega_t$ to the current seasonal effect. The other three candidate models (@sec-models) only change these matrices; the code that filters, smooths and forecasts never changes.

## Filtering, likelihood, smoothing and prediction

**Filter.** Let $a_t$ and $P_t$ be the mean and variance of the state given the data up to month $t-1$. For each month the filter computes

$$
v_t = y_t - Z^\top a_t, \quad F_t = Z^\top P_t Z + H, \quad K_t = T P_t Z / F_t, \quad
a_{t+1} = T a_t + K_t v_t, \quad P_{t+1} = T P_t T^\top - K_t K_t^\top F_t + R Q R^\top .
$$

$v_t$ is the forecast error for month $t$ made at the end of month $t-1$ (the *innovation*), $F_t$ its variance, and the Kalman gain $K_t$ says how the surprise is shared out between the level and the seasonal effects. **If month $t$ is missing** (a COVID month) there is nothing to learn, so $a_{t+1} = T a_t$ and $P_{t+1} = T P_t T^\top + R Q R^\top$: the filter carries its last view of demand forward and becomes less certain every month.

**Likelihood.** The innovations are independent, so the log likelihood is the *prediction error decomposition*

$$
\log L = -\tfrac{1}{2} \textstyle\sum_{t} \left( \log 2\pi + \log F_t + v_t^2 / F_t \right),
$$

summed over the observed months only: the COVID months contribute nothing. The initial level and seasonal effects are unknown, so I use a diffuse prior, $a_1 = 0$ and $P_1 = \kappa I$ with $\kappa = 10^7$, and leave the first $d = 13$ innovations out of the likelihood, since they only serve to learn the starting values (the large-$\kappa$ approximation to the exact diffuse filter, Durbin and Koopman, 2012, Chapter 5).

**Smoothers.** Working backwards from $r_n = 0$, $N_n = 0$ with $L_t = T - K_t Z^\top$,

$$
r_{t-1} = Z v_t / F_t + L_t^\top r_t, \qquad N_{t-1} = Z Z^\top / F_t + L_t^\top N_t L_t,
$$

(with $r_{t-1} = T^\top r_t$ and $N_{t-1} = T^\top N_t T$ for a missing month), the smoothed state is $\hat\alpha_t = a_t + P_t r_{t-1}$ with variance $V_t = P_t - P_t N_{t-1} P_t$. The *disturbance smoother* gives the smoothed state shocks $\hat\eta_t = Q R^\top r_t$ with variance $Q - Q R^\top N_t R Q$ (Durbin and Koopman, 2012, Section 4.5). Standardised, these *auxiliary residuals* are $t$-statistics for a break in the level or the seasonal pattern at month $t$.

**Prediction.** Forecasting is filtering with every future month missing: the $h$-step forecast of $y$ is $Z^\top a$ with variance $Z^\top P Z + H$, where $a$ and $P$ are propagated with the missing-month recursion.

```{r}
#| label: ssm-functions

# Kalman filter for a univariate linear Gaussian state space model ----------
# y may contain NA (missing months). Returns predicted states a_t, variances P_t,
# innovations v_t, their variances F_t and the gains K_t.
kfilter <- function(y, sys) {
  Z <- sys$Z; H <- sys$H; Tm <- sys$T
  RQR <- sys$R %*% sys$Q %*% t(sys$R)
  n <- length(y); m <- length(sys$a1)
  a <- matrix(0, n + 1, m); P <- array(0, c(m, m, n + 1))
  v <- Fv <- rep(NA_real_, n); K <- matrix(0, n, m)
  a[1, ] <- sys$a1; P[, , 1] <- sys$P1
  for (t in 1:n) {
    at <- a[t, ]; Pt <- P[, , t]
    if (is.na(y[t])) {                         # missing: prediction step only
      a[t + 1, ]   <- Tm %*% at
      P[, , t + 1] <- Tm %*% Pt %*% t(Tm) + RQR
    } else {                                   # observed: update and predict
      PZ <- Pt %*% Z
      Ft <- as.numeric(crossprod(Z, PZ)) + H
      vt <- y[t] - sum(Z * at)
      Kt <- (Tm %*% PZ) / Ft
      a[t + 1, ]   <- Tm %*% at + Kt * vt
      P[, , t + 1] <- Tm %*% Pt %*% t(Tm) - Kt %*% t(Kt) * Ft + RQR
      v[t] <- vt; Fv[t] <- Ft; K[t, ] <- Kt
    }
  }
  list(a = a, P = P, v = v, F = Fv, K = K, n = n)
}

# Log likelihood (prediction error decomposition), dropping the first d
# observed innovations because of the diffuse initialisation
kloglik <- function(kf, d) {
  ok <- which(!is.na(kf$v))
  ok <- ok[-seq_len(d)]
  -0.5 * sum(log(2 * pi) + log(kf$F[ok]) + kf$v[ok]^2 / kf$F[ok])
}

# Standardised innovations e_t = v_t / sqrt(F_t), after the diffuse period
std_innov <- function(kf, d) {
  e  <- kf$v / sqrt(kf$F)
  ok <- which(!is.na(e))
  e[ok[seq_len(d)]] <- NA
  e
}

# Kalman state smoother (Durbin and Koopman 2012, Section 4.4) ---------------
ksmooth <- function(kf, y, sys) {
  Z <- sys$Z; Tm <- sys$T; n <- kf$n; m <- length(sys$a1)
  r <- rep(0, m); N <- matrix(0, m, m)
  ahat <- matrix(0, n, m); V <- array(0, c(m, m, n))
  for (t in n:1) {
    if (is.na(y[t])) {
      r <- crossprod(Tm, r)
      N <- t(Tm) %*% N %*% Tm
    } else {
      L <- Tm - kf$K[t, ] %*% t(Z)
      r <- Z * kf$v[t] / kf$F[t] + crossprod(L, r)
      N <- Z %*% t(Z) / kf$F[t] + t(L) %*% N %*% L
    }
    Pt <- kf$P[, , t]
    ahat[t, ] <- kf$a[t, ] + Pt %*% r
    V[, , t]  <- Pt - Pt %*% N %*% Pt
  }
  list(ahat = ahat, V = V)
}

# Disturbance smoother for the state shocks (D&K Section 4.5) -----------------
# eta_hat_t = Q R' r_t, Var = Q - Q R' N_t R Q, where r_t, N_t come from t+1, ..., n
kdisturb <- function(kf, y, sys) {
  Z <- sys$Z; Tm <- sys$T; n <- kf$n; m <- length(sys$a1)
  RQ <- sys$R %*% sys$Q
  r <- rep(0, m); N <- matrix(0, m, m)
  eta <- eta_var <- matrix(NA_real_, n, ncol(sys$R))
  for (t in n:1) {
    eta[t, ]     <- crossprod(RQ, r)
    eta_var[t, ] <- diag(sys$Q - t(RQ) %*% N %*% RQ)
    if (is.na(y[t])) {
      r <- crossprod(Tm, r); N <- t(Tm) %*% N %*% Tm
    } else {
      L <- Tm - kf$K[t, ] %*% t(Z)
      r <- Z * kf$v[t] / kf$F[t] + crossprod(L, r)
      N <- Z %*% t(Z) / kf$F[t] + t(L) %*% N %*% L
    }
  }
  list(eta = eta, eta_var = eta_var)
}

# h-step forecasts: the filter run with all future months missing ------------
kforecast <- function(kf, sys, h) {
  a <- kf$a[kf$n + 1, ]; P <- kf$P[, , kf$n + 1]
  RQR <- sys$R %*% sys$Q %*% t(sys$R)
  mean <- var <- numeric(h)
  for (j in 1:h) {
    mean[j] <- sum(sys$Z * a)
    var[j]  <- as.numeric(t(sys$Z) %*% P %*% sys$Z) + sys$H
    a <- sys$T %*% a
    P <- sys$T %*% P %*% t(sys$T) + RQR
  }
  tibble(h = 1:h, mean = mean, var = var)
}
```

## Four candidate structural models {#sec-models}

All four models share the seasonal component above. They differ in the trend and in whether a short-run AR(1) component $c_t$ is added:

| Model | Trend | Extra component | States $m$ |
|:--|:--|:--|:--|
| **BSM** (basic structural model) | Local linear trend: $\mu_{t+1} = \mu_t + \nu_t + \xi_t$, $\nu_{t+1} = \nu_t + \zeta_t$ | none | 13 |
| **Level + seasonal** | Local level: $\mu_{t+1} = \mu_t + \xi_t$ | none | 12 |
| **Damped trend** | $\mu_{t+1} = \mu_t + \nu_t + \xi_t$, $\nu_{t+1} = \phi \nu_t + \zeta_t$, $0 < \phi < 1$ | none | 13 |
| **Damped trend + AR(1)** | as above | $c_{t+1} = \rho c_t + \kappa_t$ | 14 |

and in each case $y_t = \mu_t + \gamma_t\, (+\, c_t) + \varepsilon_t$. The slope $\nu_t$ is the monthly growth rate of demand. The damped trend model is the state space counterpart of the damped Holt–Winters method that did best in Project 3, and the level + seasonal model corresponds to ETS(A,N,A). The AR(1) component allows deviations from the trend that last a few months, such as a slow recovery after a shock.

```{r}
#| label: model-builder

# Build the system matrices for one of the structural models ------------------
# trend: "llt" (local linear trend), "ll" (local level), "damped"
param_names <- function(trend, ar) {
  c("eps", "level", if (trend != "ll") "slope", "seas",
    if (trend == "damped") "phi", if (ar) c("ar_var", "rho"))
}

unpack <- function(theta, trend, ar) {
  nm <- param_names(trend, ar)
  p  <- as.list(exp(theta)); names(p) <- nm
  if (trend == "damped") p$phi <- 0.98 * plogis(theta[nm == "phi"])   # phi in (0, 0.98)
  if (ar)                p$rho <- tanh(theta[nm == "rho"])             # rho in (-1, 1)
  if (trend == "ll")     p$slope <- NA
  p
}

structural_sys <- function(p, trend, ar) {
  n_tr <- if (trend == "ll") 1 else 2
  m <- n_tr + 11 + ar
  Tm <- matrix(0, m, m); Z <- numeric(m)
  Tm[1, 1] <- 1; Z[1] <- 1                                  # level
  if (n_tr == 2) {                                          # slope
    Tm[1, 2] <- 1
    Tm[2, 2] <- if (trend == "damped") p$phi else 1
  }
  s1 <- n_tr + 1                                            # seasonal block (11 states)
  Tm[s1, s1:(s1 + 10)] <- -1
  Tm[(s1 + 1):(s1 + 10), s1:(s1 + 9)] <- diag(10)
  Z[s1] <- 1
  if (ar) { Tm[m, m] <- p$rho; Z[m] <- 1 }                  # AR(1) component
  shock_rows <- c(1, if (n_tr == 2) 2, s1, if (ar) m)
  shock_var  <- c(p$level, if (n_tr == 2) p$slope, p$seas, if (ar) p$ar_var)
  R <- matrix(0, m, length(shock_rows))
  R[cbind(shock_rows, seq_along(shock_rows))] <- 1
  P1 <- diag(1e7, m)                                        # diffuse prior
  if (ar) P1[m, m] <- p$ar_var / (1 - p$rho^2)              # stationary prior for c_t
  list(Z = matrix(Z), H = p$eps, T = Tm, R = R,
       Q = diag(shock_var, length(shock_var)), a1 = rep(0, m), P1 = P1)
}

d_diffuse <- 13   # same number of dropped innovations for every model, so AIC is comparable

ssm_negll <- function(theta, y, trend, ar) {
  -kloglik(kfilter(y, structural_sys(unpack(theta, trend, ar), trend, ar)), d = d_diffuse)
}

# Maximum likelihood with several starting values (the surface is flat near the boundary)
fit_structural <- function(y, trend, ar, start = NULL) {
  nm <- param_names(trend, ar)
  th0 <- c(eps = log(1e-3), level = log(1e-3), slope = log(1e-5), seas = log(1e-5),
           phi = qlogis(0.9 / 0.98), ar_var = log(1e-3), rho = atanh(0.5))[nm]
  starts <- if (is.null(start)) list(th0, th0 + 0.5, th0 - 1) else list(start)
  best <- NULL
  for (s in starts) {
    o <- optim(s, ssm_negll, y = y, trend = trend, ar = ar, method = "BFGS",
               control = list(maxit = 500))
    o <- optim(o$par, ssm_negll, y = y, trend = trend, ar = ar, method = "Nelder-Mead",
               control = list(maxit = 4000))
    if (is.null(best) || o$value < best$value) best <- o
  }
  p   <- unpack(best$par, trend, ar)
  sys <- structural_sys(p, trend, ar)
  kf  <- kfilter(y, sys)
  list(theta = best$par, p = p, sys = sys, kf = kf, loglik = -best$value,
       k = length(nm), trend = trend, ar = ar, converged = best$convergence == 0)
}
```

## Profile likelihoods: how well do the data pin down the variances?

The individual variances control the filter's behaviour, so it matters how precisely they are estimated. For the selected model I compute the *profile log likelihood* of each variance: fix it on a grid and maximise over the other two. A 95% confidence interval is the set of values whose profile is within $\chi^2_{1, 0.95}/2 = 1.92$ of the maximum, which, unlike a Wald interval, works on the boundary.

```{r}
#| label: profile
#| cache: true

nm_sel <- param_names(sel$trend, sel$ar)
profile_par <- function(which_par, grid) {
  free <- setdiff(seq_along(nm_sel), which_par)
  start <- sel$theta[free]
  sapply(grid, function(val) {
    f <- function(th) { full <- numeric(length(nm_sel)); full[free] <- th
                        full[which_par] <- log(val); ssm_negll(full, y_tour, sel$trend, sel$ar) }
    o <- optim(start, f, method = "Nelder-Mead", control = list(maxit = 2000))
    start <<- o$par
    -o$value
  })
}
```

The three variances behave very differently (@fig-profile):

* **Level variance: well determined.** Its 95% profile interval runs from `r sci(pci("Level", "lo"))` to `r sci(pci("Level", "hi"))`, so the monthly standard deviation of underlying demand is between `r pct(sqrt(pci("Level", "lo")), 1)` and `r pct(sqrt(pci("Level", "hi")), 1)`. The data are informative about how fast demand moves.
* **Seasonal variance: moderately determined**, between `r sci(pci("Seasonal", "lo"))` and `r sci(pci("Seasonal", "hi"))`, so the seasonal pattern certainly changes, but the rate is known only to within a factor of about `r fmt(pci("Seasonal", "hi") / pci("Seasonal", "lo"), 0)`.
* **Irregular variance: on the boundary.** The profile is flat from zero up to about `r sci(pci("Irregular", "hi"))` and then falls away. The data cannot distinguish "no month-specific noise" from "a little", but they rule out a large irregular component. This is why the estimate is essentially zero, and why a Wald interval based on the curvature at the maximum would be meaningless here.

For the filter, the flat irregular profile means that the share of each surprise treated as noise rather than signal is uncertain at the low end, but small in any case.

```{r}
fit_pre <- fit_structural(log(pre_df$nights), sel$trend, sel$ar)
K_pre   <- fit_pre$kf$K[nrow(pre_df), ]

ets_pre <- pre_df |> model(ETS(log(nights) ~ error("A") + trend("N") + season("A")))
ets_par <- tidy(ets_pre) |> select(term, estimate) |> deframe()
```

Once the gain has settled to a constant $\bar K$, the filter is $a_{t+1} = T a_t + \bar K v_t$: a fixed share of each surprise goes to the level and a fixed share to the seasonal effect. That is exactly the error-correction form of additive Holt–Winters, ETS(A,N,A), with $\alpha$ the level share and $\gamma$ (roughly) the seasonal share. For the selected model the steady-state gain gives **`r pct(steady_K[1])` of each surprise to the level and `r pct(steady_K[g_sel])` to that calendar month's seasonal effect**, adding to `r pct(steady_K[1] + steady_K[g_sel])` because there is no irregular component to absorb any of it.

To compare like with like I fit both models to the pre-COVID months only (ETS cannot handle the gap). The state space model's steady-state gain is `r fmt(K_pre[1], 2)` for the level and `r fmt(K_pre[g_sel], 2)` for the seasonal effect; ETS(A,N,A) estimates $\alpha$ = `r fmt(ets_par["alpha"], 2)` and $\gamma$ = `r fmt(ets_par["gamma"], 2)`. The split between level and seasonal differs, but both add to about one: both methods conclude that a surprise in accommodation demand is almost never a one-off. The two estimators differ because ETS estimates the smoothing parameters directly by least squares on one-step errors with the initial states as free parameters, while the state space model derives them from the variances, with a diffuse start. This is the link between Projects 3 and 4: the smoothing parameters of exponential smoothing are a steady-state Kalman gain, determined by the signal-to-noise ratios.

# Simulation study: can the lost-demand estimate be trusted?

The lost-demand figure relies on the smoother bridging a 25-month gap, on variances estimated from 76 innovations, and on intervals that assume the variances are known. To check these in this exact setting, I simulate 300 series of the same length from the fitted `r best_aic` model, starting from the smoothed state in January 2015, delete the same 25 months, re-estimate the model with my code, and compare the estimates with the simulated truth.

```{r}

impute_covid <- function(df) {
  n_win <- month_num(covid_end) - month_num(covid_start) + 2
  df <- df |>
    as_tibble() |>
    mutate(t = row_number(), mon = factor(month(as.Date(Month))),
           in_win = Month >= covid_start & Month <= covid_end,
           ramp = pmin(pmax((month_num(Month) - month_num(covid_start) + 1) / n_win, 0), 1),
           y_obs = if_else(in_win, NA_real_, log(nights)))
  fit <- lm(y_obs ~ t + mon + ramp, data = df)
  df |>
    mutate(nights_imp = if_else(in_win, exp(predict(fit, newdata = df)), nights)) |>
    select(Month, nights, nights_imp, period)
}
```

# Discussion

## Key findings {#sec-findings}

1. **My implementation is correct on this series.** It matches the base R Kalman routines on the accommodation data with the COVID months missing (largest state difference `r sci(diff_state)`), and it reproduces the exact log likelihood of the Project 1 airline SARIMA (`r fmt(ll_at_arima$ll, 4)` against `r fmt(fit_airline$loglik, 4)` from `arima()`).
2. **Likelihood.** The `r best_aic` model has the lowest AIC, but the four structural models and the airline SARIMA are within a few log likelihood units of each other. The level variance is well determined (monthly SD of underlying demand `r pct(sqrt(pci("Level", "lo")), 1)` to `r pct(sqrt(pci("Level", "hi")), 1)`), the irregular variance sits on the boundary at zero, and April 2022 is by far the least likely month.
3. **Filtering.** Nearly every surprise in accommodation demand is treated as lasting: the steady-state gain gives `r pct(steady_K[1])` to the level and `r pct(steady_K[g_sel])` to the seasonal pattern, matching the near-one smoothing parameters of exponential smoothing. Through the gap the filter's uncertainty about demand grew `r fmt(sd_restart / sd_feb20, 1)`-fold; on restart demand was `r pct(1 - exp(act_restart - pred_restart))` below the filter's prediction, and the filter re-learned the level within a few months without being told anything about COVID.
4. **Smoothing.** About `r fmt(lost_smooth$lost / 1000, 1)` million stay unit nights (`r pct(lost_smooth$share)` of expected demand) were lost between March 2020 and March 2022, against `r fmt(lost_filter$lost / 1000, 1)` million if the February 2020 forecast is used as the counterfactual. Across the four models the estimate varies by `r fmt((max(lost_tbl$lost) - min(lost_tbl$lost)) / 1000, 1)` million nights. In simulations from the fitted model the smoothed counterfactual is within about `r pct(cf_spread, 0)` of the truth in 90% of cases.
5. **Prediction.** On the test set the best state space model is `r best_ssm_test` (RMSE `r fmt(rg(best_ssm_test, "RMSE"))`, against `r fmt(rg("SARIMA (Project 1)", "RMSE"))` for the SARIMA and `r fmt(rg("Damped HW (Project 3)", "RMSE"))` for damped Holt–Winters), and the BSM with its fixed slope is the worst (RMSE `r fmt(rg("BSM", "RMSE"))`).

## Issues and limitations

* **The counterfactual depends on the model.** Inside the gap the smoother interpolates with a random-walk level, so the no-COVID path is close to a straight line between the pre- and post-COVID levels. The filter-versus-smoother comparison shows that the estimate of demand lost hinges on whether the lower post-COVID level is treated as something that would have happened anyway.
* **One set of variances for the whole decade.** The innovations are more spread out after COVID than before, so the post-COVID period may need larger variances; the restart month alone has a large influence on the level variance.
* **Moving holidays.** Easter and school holidays move between March and April, and the model reads this as level or seasonal shocks.
* **Approximate diffuse initialisation and boundary estimates.** Using $\kappa = 10^7$ and dropping 13 innovations uses up more than a year of data, and with the irregular variance on the boundary, AIC and likelihood ratio comparisons behave non-standardly.
* **Intervals ignore parameter uncertainty.** The smoothing and forecast intervals treat the variances as known; the simulation study shows how much this matters for the total lost demand.
* **Same test window throughout.** All projects use one test window and a short cross-validation period, both in the post-COVID recovery, which favours models that do not extrapolate the pre-2020 decline.

## What I would do differently

I would implement the exact diffuse Kalman filter, add the Project 3 intervention terms and an Easter regressor as regression effects inside the state space model (so the COVID months could be used rather than skipped and the moving holiday would no longer be read as a shock), and let the variances differ before and after COVID. A Bayesian treatment of the variances, with priors that keep the irregular variance off the boundary, would carry the parameter uncertainty into the lost-demand estimate, and a simulation smoother would give a proper interval for the total demand lost.
