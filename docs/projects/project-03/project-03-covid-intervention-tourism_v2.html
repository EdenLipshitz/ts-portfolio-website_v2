Executive summary

NoteExecutive summary

Title: Modelling the COVID-19 shock in South African accommodation demand

Section and topic: Section 2. Exponential smoothing (ETS), regression with ARIMA errors and intervention (transfer function) terms, state-space models and the Kalman filter.

Type of project: Model comparison on real-world data, with a profile likelihood search for the intervention decay rate. It extends Project 1 on the same series and the same test set, so the results of the two projects can be compared directly.

Question: In Project 1 the COVID-19 months were either kept, which broke the SARIMA model, or deleted, which threw information away. Can the shock be modelled instead, and does that, or adding the exchange rate as a predictor, give better forecasts of accommodation demand than ETS and the Project 1 SARIMA?

Data: Real-world, publicly available data. Statistics South Africa, Tourist Accommodation release P6410, series B64100 (stay unit nights sold, total industry), and the monthly average rand per US dollar exchange rate (FRED series EXSFUS), January 2015 to June 2026. Full source details are in Section 2.1.

Methods: ETS; regression with ARIMA errors containing deterministic seasonal dummies and COVID intervention terms (a geometrically decaying shock, two pulses and a level shift), with the decay rate chosen by profile likelihood; the same model with the log exchange rate added, forecast both ex ante and ex post; a basic structural model (local level, slope and seasonal) fitted by maximum likelihood with the Kalman filter, with the COVID months treated as missing. All models go through the Box and Jenkins loop and are compared with the Project 1 SARIMA and the seasonal naive benchmark on the July 2024 to June 2026 test set.

Key findings: See Section 8.1.

What I would do differently: Use rolling-origin cross-validation for the final comparison, and estimate the decay rate jointly with the other parameters rather than on a grid.

Detailed analysis: the sections below.

1 Introduction and research question

Project 1 showed that the COVID-19 collapse in April 2020 is the main obstacle to forecasting South African accommodation demand. Keeping the COVID months in a SARIMA model inflated the residual variance many times over and pushed the seasonal MA coefficient to the invertibility boundary. Treating the months as missing solved this, but at the cost of discarding two years of data. This project asks whether the shock can be modelled directly, using the Section 2 tools:

  • ETS as a baseline: exponential smoothing adapts its level quickly, which might let it recover from the shock without any special treatment.
  • Regression with ARIMA errors and intervention terms: the shock enters as explanatory variables, so the months are used rather than deleted, and the size and persistence of the shock are estimated.
  • A basic structural model (BSM): a state-space model with an evolving level, slope and seasonal pattern. The Kalman filter handles missing observations naturally, so the COVID months can be skipped without breaking the time ordering, and the Kalman smoother estimates the underlying level through the gap.

A second question is whether the exchange rate helps. A weaker rand makes South Africa cheaper for foreign visitors, so the rand might carry information about demand. This links to Project 2, which models the rand’s volatility.

Research question. Does modelling the COVID-19 shock explicitly (with intervention terms or as missing data in a state-space model), or adding the exchange rate, forecast monthly accommodation demand better than ETS and the Project 1 SARIMA?

3 Model 1: Exponential smoothing (ETS)

ETS models describe a series through its level, trend and seasonal components, each updated by exponential smoothing. The ETS() function in fable selects the error, trend and seasonal forms (additive, multiplicative or none) by AICc. ETS cannot handle missing values, so it is fitted to the full training series including the COVID months. This makes ETS a useful baseline: it shows how far a method that adapts its level quickly can cope with the shock on its own.

4 Model 2: Regression with ARIMA errors and intervention terms

4.1 Why deterministic seasonality

In Project 1, every seasonal ARIMA model fitted with the COVID months included estimated a seasonal MA coefficient at or near -1. A seasonal MA factor \((1 - L^{12})\) that cancels the seasonal difference \((1 - L^{12})\) is the classic sign that the seasonal pattern is fixed rather than evolving, so that seasonal differencing is over-differencing. The seasonal plots in Project 1 point the same way. In this model the seasonality is therefore represented by 11 monthly dummy variables, while a first difference in the errors handles the wandering level.

4.2 Modelling the shock

The intervention terms follow the transfer function idea from Chapter 2: the effect of an event is described by a small set of explanatory variables whose shape reflects how the event unfolded. With \(t_0\) = April 2020 and everything on the log scale:

  • Decaying shock: \(D_t(\delta) = \delta^{\,t - t_0}\) for \(t \ge t_0\) and 0 before. Its coefficient \(\omega\) is the immediate effect in April 2020, which then dies out geometrically at rate \(\delta\). This is the transfer function \(\omega / (1 - \delta L)\) applied to a pulse at \(t_0\).
  • Pulses for March 2020 (lockdown started on 27 March, so the month was only partly affected) and May 2020 (the industry stayed almost completely closed, so the bottom of the collapse lasted two months rather than one).
  • Level shift: \(S_t = 1\) for \(t \ge t_0\), allowing a permanent difference between the pre-COVID and post-COVID levels.

The model is

\[ \log y_t = \sum_{j=1}^{11} \gamma_j M_{jt} + \omega D_t(\delta) + \kappa_1 P^{\text{Mar20}}_t + \kappa_2 P^{\text{May20}}_t + \lambda S_t + \eta_t, \qquad (1 - L)\,\phi(L)\,\eta_t = \theta(L)\,\varepsilon_t , \]

where \(M_{jt}\) are monthly dummies and \(\eta_t\) follows an ARIMA(\(p\), 1, \(q\)) process.

Code 
covid_vars <- function(df, delta) {
  k0 <- month_num(shock_month)
  df |>
    mutate(k     = month_num(Month) - k0,
           decay = if_else(k >= 0, delta^pmax(k, 0), 0),
           mar20 = as.numeric(k == -1),
           may20 = as.numeric(k == 1),
           step  = as.numeric(k >= 0))
}

4.3 Choosing the decay rate by profile likelihood

The decay rate \(\delta\) enters the model non-linearly, so it cannot be estimated by ARIMA() directly. Instead I profile it: for each \(\delta\) on a grid from 0.70 to 0.96, the model is fitted (with the ARIMA error orders chosen by AICc) and the AICc recorded. The value of \(\delta\) with the smallest AICc is used. Because \(\delta\) is estimated, the model really has one more parameter than ARIMA() reports; this does not change the ranking along the grid.

Code 
delta_grid <- seq(0.70, 0.96, by = 0.02)

profile <- bind_rows(lapply(delta_grid, function(dl) {
  f <- covid_vars(train, dl) |>
    model(reg = ARIMA(log(nights) ~ season() + decay + mar20 + may20 + step +
                        pdq(d = 1) + PDQ(0, 0, 0)))
  tibble(delta = dl,
         AICc  = glance(f)$AICc,
         spec  = fabletools::model_sum(f$reg[[1]]$fit))
}))

delta_hat <- profile$delta[which.min(profile$AICc)]

4.6 Does the exchange rate help?

Because the model is in logs, the coefficient on log USD/ZAR is an elasticity: a 10% weakening of the rand is associated with a change of about 2.7% in nights sold, holding the seasonal and COVID terms fixed. The estimate is 0.270 (p 0.175), and the AICc gets worse from -227.1 to -225.9. The exchange rate does not add significant information once the seasonal pattern and the COVID shock are accounted for, which is not surprising since most stays are domestic and the effect of the rand on foreign arrivals works with a lag. The model is in first differences, so the coefficient measures the effect of changes in the rand on changes in demand within the same month.

A model with an explanatory variable has a practical catch: to forecast nights sold, I need future values of the exchange rate, which are not known when the forecast is made. Two versions are therefore evaluated:

  • Ex ante (realistic): the exchange rate is assumed to stay at its last observed value (June 2024), a naive forecast.
  • Ex post (optimistic): the actual exchange rate during the test period is used. This is not a genuine forecast, since it uses information that would not have been available in June 2024. It shows the best the model could do if the rand were forecast perfectly.

5 Model 3: Basic structural model with the Kalman filter

The basic structural model (BSM) writes the log series as the sum of unobserved components, each evolving as a random walk:

\[ \begin{aligned} \log y_t &= \mu_t + s_t + \varepsilon_t, & \varepsilon_t &\sim N(0, \sigma^2_\varepsilon) \\ \mu_{t} &= \mu_{t-1} + \nu_{t-1} + \xi_t, & \xi_t &\sim N(0, \sigma^2_\xi) \\ \nu_{t} &= \nu_{t-1} + \zeta_t, & \zeta_t &\sim N(0, \sigma^2_\zeta) \\ s_t &= -\textstyle\sum_{j=1}^{11} s_{t-j} + \omega_t, & \omega_t &\sim N(0, \sigma^2_\omega) \end{aligned} \]

The first line is the observation equation; the others are the state equations for the level \(\mu_t\), the slope \(\nu_t\) and the seasonal effect \(s_t\). The four variances are estimated by maximum likelihood, where the likelihood comes from the prediction error decomposition computed by the Kalman filter. The same model can be built in dlm as dlmModPoly(2) + dlmModSeas(12); here I use the base R function StructTS(), which fits exactly this model.

The COVID months are set to missing (as in Design B of Project 1). At a missing observation the Kalman filter performs only the prediction step, so the states are carried through the gap and the likelihood is computed from the observed months only.

Two versions are fitted: the full BSM, and a version with the slope variance fixed at zero (a fixed drift), which is a restriction suggested by the size of the estimated slope variance.

The estimated slope variance in the full BSM is tiny (5.2e-07), and fixing it at zero changes the log likelihood by only 0.08. The restricted model even has a slightly higher log likelihood, which should be impossible for nested models fitted by maximum likelihood: the optimiser for the full model stopped slightly short of the maximum. Variances close to zero sit on the boundary of the parameter space, where the likelihood is very flat and numerical optimisation is unreliable, which is a known difficulty with structural models. The seasonal variance is small compared with the level variance, which agrees with the finding that the seasonal pattern is very stable. As the forecasts below show, the tiny slope variance nevertheless matters for forecasting: it lets the slope adapt to the steep post-COVID recovery, and that recovery growth is then extrapolated into the test period.

Diagnostics. The standardised one-step prediction residuals (innovations) of a correctly specified state-space model should be white noise. The Ljung and Box test on 24 lags gives p = < 0.001 for the full BSM and p = < 0.001 for the fixed slope version. Both models fail: there is clear autocorrelation left in the innovations (Figure 7). The irregular variance is estimated at or near zero, so all short-run variation has to be absorbed by the random walk level, and the model has no way to represent the short-lived, autocorrelated deviations that the ARIMA error in the regression model captures. By the Box and Jenkins rule, the BSM should not be used to forecast without being extended (for example with an AR(1) component), which the StructTS() function does not allow. Its forecasts are still evaluated below, but as a reference rather than a candidate.

6 Forecast evaluation

All models produce forecasts for July 2024 to June 2026 from the end of June 2024, with 80% and 95% prediction intervals. 6.2 Results

Overall ranking. The lowest test RMSE is achieved by the Reg model (98.8 thousand nights). Among the models that passed the diagnostic step, the best is the Reg model. For reference, the Project 1 SARIMA has an RMSE of 101.6 and the seasonal naive benchmark 132.6.

ETS. ETS reaches an RMSE of 130.0, beating the seasonal naive benchmark even though it was fitted to data that include the COVID collapse. Its fast-moving level lets it recover from the shock without any special treatment, which a SARIMA with the COVID months included could not do in Project 1. Its 95% intervals are, however, wider than those of the regression model, because the COVID months still inflate its estimated error variance (mean width 2,292 against 1,060 thousand nights).

Regression with intervention terms. Modelling the shock explicitly gives an RMSE of 98.8 and a MAPE of 4.51%, better than the Project 1 SARIMA that simply deleted the COVID months. Its intervals cover 100% of the test months at the 95% level and 100% at the 80% level. The two approaches reach very similar forecast accuracy, which makes sense: by June 2024 the temporary part of the shock had almost disappeared (\(\hat\delta^{\,50} \approx\) 1.1e-06), so the main forecasting information in both models comes from the post-COVID level and the seasonal pattern. The advantage of the regression model is not mainly accuracy but interpretation: it estimates the size, shape and persistence of the shock (Figure 4) instead of discarding it.

Exchange rate. Adding the rand changes the RMSE to 106.2 ex ante and 130.3 ex post. Neither version improves on the model without the exchange rate. The gap between the ex ante and ex post results is a reminder that a regression model’s forecasts are only as good as the forecasts of its explanatory variables.

State-space models. The full BSM has an RMSE of 184.7 and the fixed slope version 102.9. The difference comes from the slope: in the full model the slope adapts to the steep recovery in 2022 and 2023 and this growth is extrapolated forward, whereas with a fixed slope the long-run average growth is used. This shows that two models with almost identical likelihoods can give very different forecasts, because the likelihood is dominated by one-step-ahead fit while forecasts two years ahead depend on how the trend is extrapolated. Both BSMs failed the residual diagnostics, so even if their forecasts happen to be competitive on this test set, they are not candidates under the Box and Jenkins rule.

8 Discussion

8.1 Key findings

  1. Deterministic seasonality. The seasonal MA coefficient of -1 found in Project 1 is evidence of a fixed seasonal pattern. Replacing seasonal differencing with monthly dummies gave a regression model with white noise residuals (Ljung and Box p = 0.556).
  2. The shock can be modelled parsimoniously. A decaying intervention with \(\hat\delta\) = 0.76 (half-life about 2.5 months), two pulses and a level shift capture the collapse and recovery with five parameters instead of 25 deleted months.
  3. Forecast accuracy. The best model on the test set was Reg, and the best model that also passed the diagnostics was Reg. The regression model (RMSE 98.8) and the Project 1 SARIMA (RMSE 101.6) were close, because by 2024 the temporary part of the shock had died out.
  4. ETS is a robust baseline. Without any special treatment of COVID, ETS reached an RMSE of 130.0, because its level adapts quickly.
  5. The exchange rate had an estimated elasticity of 0.270 (p 0.175). Ex ante, it did not improve the forecasts.
  6. The BSM interpolates the level through the COVID gap and its forecasts depend strongly on whether the slope is allowed to vary, but its innovations were not white noise, so it failed the Box and Jenkins diagnostic step.

8.2 Issues and limitations

  • Single test period. All comparisons rest on one 24 month test set. Project 1 showed that rolling-origin cross-validation gives a more reliable ranking; with more time I would repeat it here, although refitting the BSM and repeating the \(\delta\) profile at every origin is slow.
  • Choice of intervention shape. The decay, pulses and step were chosen by looking at the data, so there is a risk of over-fitting the shape of the shock to this particular series. The AICc profile guards against some of this, but the pulse months were picked by eye.
  • Confounding with the exchange rate. The rand depreciated sharply in March and April 2020, at the same time as the lockdown. Adding the exchange rate after the intervention terms avoids attributing the COVID collapse to the rand, but it also means the exchange rate effect is estimated mainly from the non-COVID months.
  • Same-month effect only. The exchange rate enters without a lag. Foreign visitors book weeks or months ahead, so a lagged effect would be more realistic and would also make ex ante forecasting easier for short horizons.
  • BSM limitations in StructTS(). The irregular variance was estimated at zero and the innovations were autocorrelated. A more flexible state-space model (for example in dlm or KFAS, with an AR(1) component or explanatory variables in the state equation) would be the natural next step.
  • Profile likelihood standard errors. Treating \(\hat\delta\) as known when computing the other standard errors understates their uncertainty.

8.3 What I would do differently

I would estimate \(\delta\) jointly with the other parameters by maximising the likelihood directly, use rolling-origin cross-validation for the final ranking, try a lagged exchange rate, and build the state-space model in dlm so that an AR(1) component and the intervention terms could be added to it, combining the strengths of the regression and state-space approaches in one model.

# Introduction and research question

Project 1 showed that the COVID-19 collapse in April 2020 is the main obstacle to forecasting South African accommodation demand. Keeping the COVID months in a SARIMA model inflated the residual variance many times over and pushed the seasonal MA coefficient to the invertibility boundary. Treating the months as missing solved this, but at the cost of discarding two years of data. This project asks whether the shock can be modelled directly, using the Section 2 tools:

* **ETS** as a baseline: exponential smoothing adapts its level quickly, which might let it recover from the shock without any special treatment.
* **Regression with ARIMA errors and intervention terms:** the shock enters as explanatory variables, so the months are used rather than deleted, and the size and persistence of the shock are estimated.
* **A basic structural model (BSM):** a state-space model with an evolving level, slope and seasonal pattern. The Kalman filter handles missing observations naturally, so the COVID months can be skipped without breaking the time ordering, and the Kalman smoother estimates the underlying level through the gap.

A second question is whether the exchange rate helps. A weaker rand makes South Africa cheaper for foreign visitors, so the rand might carry information about demand. This links to Project 2, which models the rand's volatility.

**Research question.** *Does modelling the COVID-19 shock explicitly (with intervention terms or as missing data in a state-space model), or adding the exchange rate, forecast monthly accommodation demand better than ETS and the Project 1 SARIMA?*

# Model 2: Regression with ARIMA errors and intervention terms

## Why deterministic seasonality

In Project 1, every seasonal ARIMA model fitted with the COVID months included estimated a seasonal MA coefficient at or near -1. A seasonal MA factor $(1 - L^{12})$ that cancels the seasonal difference $(1 - L^{12})$ is the classic sign that the seasonal pattern is fixed rather than evolving, so that seasonal differencing is over-differencing. The seasonal plots in Project 1 point the same way. In this model the seasonality is therefore represented by 11 monthly dummy variables, while a first difference in the errors handles the wandering level.

## Modelling the shock

The intervention terms follow the transfer function idea from Chapter 2: the effect of an event is described by a small set of explanatory variables whose shape reflects how the event unfolded. With $t_0$ = April 2020 and everything on the log scale:

* **Decaying shock:** $D_t(\delta) = \delta^{\,t - t_0}$ for $t \ge t_0$ and 0 before. Its coefficient $\omega$ is the immediate effect in April 2020, which then dies out geometrically at rate $\delta$. This is the transfer function $\omega / (1 - \delta L)$ applied to a pulse at $t_0$.
* **Pulses** for March 2020 (lockdown started on 27 March, so the month was only partly affected) and May 2020 (the industry stayed almost completely closed, so the bottom of the collapse lasted two months rather than one).
* **Level shift:** $S_t = 1$ for $t \ge t_0$, allowing a permanent difference between the pre-COVID and post-COVID levels.

The model is

$$
\log y_t = \sum_{j=1}^{11} \gamma_j M_{jt} + \omega D_t(\delta) + \kappa_1 P^{\text{Mar20}}_t + \kappa_2 P^{\text{May20}}_t + \lambda S_t + \eta_t, \qquad (1 - L)\,\phi(L)\,\eta_t = \theta(L)\,\varepsilon_t ,
$$

where $M_{jt}$ are monthly dummies and $\eta_t$ follows an ARIMA($p$, 1, $q$) process.

```{r}
#| label: covid-vars

covid_vars <- function(df, delta) {
  k0 <- month_num(shock_month)
  df |>
    mutate(k     = month_num(Month) - k0,
           decay = if_else(k >= 0, delta^pmax(k, 0), 0),
           mar20 = as.numeric(k == -1),
           may20 = as.numeric(k == 1),
           step  = as.numeric(k >= 0))
}
```

## Choosing the decay rate by profile likelihood

The decay rate $\delta$ enters the model non-linearly, so it cannot be estimated by `ARIMA()` directly. Instead I profile it: for each $\delta$ on a grid from 0.70 to 0.96, the model is fitted (with the ARIMA error orders chosen by AICc) and the AICc recorded. The value of $\delta$ with the smallest AICc is used. Because $\delta$ is estimated, the model really has one more parameter than `ARIMA()` reports; this does not change the ranking along the grid.

```{r}
#| label: profile-delta

delta_grid <- seq(0.70, 0.96, by = 0.02)

  f <- covid_vars(train, dl) |>
    model(reg = ARIMA(log(nights) ~ season() + decay + mar20 + may20 + step +
                        pdq(d = 1) + PDQ(0, 0, 0)))
  tibble(delta = dl,
         AICc  = glance(f)$AICc,
         spec  = fabletools::model_sum(f$reg[[1]]$fit))

delta_hat <- profile$delta[which.min(profile$AICc)]
```

# Model 3: Basic structural model with the Kalman filter

The basic structural model (BSM) writes the log series as the sum of unobserved components, each evolving as a random walk:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\log y_t &= \mu_t + s_t + \varepsilon_t, & \varepsilon_t &\sim N(0, \sigma^2_\varepsilon) \\
\mu_{t} &= \mu_{t-1} + \nu_{t-1} + \xi_t, & \xi_t &\sim N(0, \sigma^2_\xi) \\
\nu_{t} &= \nu_{t-1} + \zeta_t, & \zeta_t &\sim N(0, \sigma^2_\zeta) \\
s_t &= -\textstyle\sum_{j=1}^{11} s_{t-j} + \omega_t, & \omega_t &\sim N(0, \sigma^2_\omega)
\end{aligned}
$$

The first line is the observation equation; the others are the state equations for the level $\mu_t$, the slope $\nu_t$ and the seasonal effect $s_t$. The four variances are estimated by maximum likelihood, where the likelihood comes from the prediction error decomposition computed by the Kalman filter. The same model can be built in `dlm` as `dlmModPoly(2) + dlmModSeas(12)`; here I use the base R function `StructTS()`, which fits exactly this model.

The COVID months are set to missing (as in Design B of Project 1). At a missing observation the Kalman filter performs only the prediction step, so the states are carried through the gap and the likelihood is computed from the observed months only.

Two versions are fitted: the full BSM, and a version with the slope variance fixed at zero (a fixed drift), which is a restriction suggested by the size of the estimated slope variance.

**Exchange rate.** Adding the rand changes the RMSE to `r fmt(rg("Reg + ZAR (ex ante)", "RMSE"))` ex ante and `r fmt(rg("Reg + ZAR (ex post)", "RMSE"))` ex post. `r if (rg("Reg + ZAR (ex post)", "RMSE") < rg("Reg", "RMSE") && rg("Reg + ZAR (ex ante)", "RMSE") >= rg("Reg", "RMSE")) "The exchange rate only helps when its future values are known, which is never the case in practice, so the realistic (ex ante) model is not an improvement." else if (rg("Reg + ZAR (ex ante)", "RMSE") < rg("Reg", "RMSE")) "Even the realistic ex ante version improves on the model without the exchange rate, although with a single test period this difference should be interpreted cautiously." else "Neither version improves on the model without the exchange rate."` The gap between the ex ante and ex post results is a reminder that a regression model's forecasts are only as good as the forecasts of its explanatory variables.

**State-space models.** The full BSM has an RMSE of `r fmt(rg("BSM", "RMSE"))` and the fixed slope version `r fmt(rg("BSM (fixed slope)", "RMSE"))`. The difference comes from the slope: in the full model the slope adapts to the steep recovery in 2022 and 2023 and this growth is extrapolated forward, whereas with a fixed slope the long-run average growth is used. This shows that two models with almost identical likelihoods can give very different forecasts, because the likelihood is dominated by one-step-ahead fit while forecasts two years ahead depend on how the trend is extrapolated. `r if (max(lb_free, lb_fixed) < 0.05) "Both BSMs failed the residual diagnostics, so even if their forecasts happen to be competitive on this test set, they are not candidates under the Box and Jenkins rule." else ""`

# Discussion

## Key findings {#sec-findings}

1. **Deterministic seasonality.** The seasonal MA coefficient of -1 found in Project 1 is evidence of a fixed seasonal pattern. Replacing seasonal differencing with monthly dummies gave a regression model with white noise residuals (Ljung and Box p = `r pfmt(lbp("Reg"))`).
2. **The shock can be modelled parsimoniously.** A decaying intervention with $\hat\delta$ = `r fmt(delta_hat, 2)` (half-life about `r fmt(log(0.5) / log(delta_hat), 1)` months), two pulses and a level shift capture the collapse and recovery with five parameters instead of 25 deleted months.
3. **Forecast accuracy.** The best model on the test set was `r best_all`, and the best model that also passed the diagnostics was `r best_cand`. The regression model (RMSE `r fmt(rg("Reg", "RMSE"))`) and the Project 1 SARIMA (RMSE `r fmt(rg("SARIMA (Project 1)", "RMSE"))`) were close, because by 2024 the temporary part of the shock had died out.
4. **ETS is a robust baseline.** Without any special treatment of COVID, ETS reached an RMSE of `r fmt(rg("ETS", "RMSE"))`, because its level adapts quickly.
5. **The exchange rate** had an estimated elasticity of `r fmt(b_zar, 3)` (p `r pfmt(p_zar)`). Ex ante, it `r if (rg("Reg + ZAR (ex ante)", "RMSE") < rg("Reg", "RMSE")) "improved" else "did not improve"` the forecasts.
6. **The BSM** interpolates the level through the COVID gap and its forecasts depend strongly on whether the slope is allowed to vary, but its innovations `r if (max(lb_free, lb_fixed) < 0.05) "were not white noise, so it failed the Box and Jenkins diagnostic step" else "passed the white noise test"`.

## Issues and limitations

* **Single test period.** All comparisons rest on one 24 month test set. Project 1 showed that rolling-origin cross-validation gives a more reliable ranking; with more time I would repeat it here, although refitting the BSM and repeating the $\delta$ profile at every origin is slow.
* **Choice of intervention shape.** The decay, pulses and step were chosen by looking at the data, so there is a risk of over-fitting the shape of the shock to this particular series. The AICc profile guards against some of this, but the pulse months were picked by eye.
* **Confounding with the exchange rate.** The rand depreciated sharply in March and April 2020, at the same time as the lockdown. Adding the exchange rate after the intervention terms avoids attributing the COVID collapse to the rand, but it also means the exchange rate effect is estimated mainly from the non-COVID months.
* **Same-month effect only.** The exchange rate enters without a lag. Foreign visitors book weeks or months ahead, so a lagged effect would be more realistic and would also make ex ante forecasting easier for short horizons.
* **BSM limitations in `StructTS()`.** The irregular variance was estimated at zero and the innovations were autocorrelated. A more flexible state-space model (for example in `dlm` or `KFAS`, with an AR(1) component or explanatory variables in the state equation) would be the natural next step.
* **Profile likelihood standard errors.** Treating $\hat\delta$ as known when computing the other standard errors understates their uncertainty.

## What I would do differently

I would estimate $\delta$ jointly with the other parameters by maximising the likelihood directly, use rolling-origin cross-validation for the final ranking, try a lagged exchange rate, and build the state-space model in `dlm` so that an AR(1) component and the intervention terms could be added to it, combining the strengths of the regression and state-space approaches in one model.
