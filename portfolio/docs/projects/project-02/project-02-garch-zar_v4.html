NoteExecutive summary

Title: Volatility of the South African rand with ARCH and GARCH models

Section and topic: Section 1. Conditional variance models: stylised facts of returns, testing for ARCH effects, ARCH, GARCH and asymmetric GARCH models, volatility forecasting.

Type of project: Implementation (own maximum likelihood code for GARCH(1,1), checked against rugarch and by a small simulation study) and model comparison on real-world data. The Value-at-Risk backtest is an idea not covered in the lectures.

Question: Do daily USD/ZAR exchange rate returns show volatility clustering, and which conditional variance model gives the most reliable one-day-ahead risk forecasts?

Data: Real-world, publicly available data: the South African rand to US dollar spot exchange rate (FRED series DEXSFUS, Board of Governors of the Federal Reserve System), daily, January 2010 to September 2026. Full source details are in Section 2.1.

Methods: Exploratory analysis of the stylised facts; Ljung and Box and ARCH LM tests; the Box and Jenkins loop applied to the mean and the variance equation; ARCH(1), GARCH(1,1) with normal and Student t errors, and GJR-GARCH(1,1) with Student t errors; my own implementation of the GARCH(1,1) likelihood; a rolling out-of-sample evaluation of 1000 one-day-ahead forecasts, scored by Value-at-Risk backtests (Kupiec and Christoffersen tests), quantile loss and QLIKE, against historical simulation and constant variance benchmarks.

Key findings: The returns show all the stylised facts: almost no autocorrelation in the returns themselves, strong autocorrelation in squared returns, and fat tails. ARCH(1) fails the residual diagnostics, while the GARCH(1,1) models capture the clustering with a very persistent variance. Student t errors fit far better than normal errors. The detailed results of the backtest, including which models give correctly calibrated VaR, are in Section 9.1.

What I would do differently: Use a longer test period so that the 1% VaR tests have more power, and compare GARCH with a regime-switching model of volatility (Project 4).

Detailed analysis: the sections below.

1 Introduction and research question

Exchange rate returns behave very differently from the seasonal series in Project 1. The level of the rand is close to a random walk, so its changes are almost unpredictable, but the size of those changes is not: calm periods and turbulent periods come in clusters. ARIMA models describe the conditional mean and assume a constant variance, so they cannot capture this. ARCH and GARCH models add a second equation for the conditional variance.

The rand is a useful series for this because it is one of the most traded and most volatile emerging market currencies, and its history since 2010 includes several clear shocks, for example the dismissal of the finance minister in December 2015 and the start of the COVID-19 pandemic in March 2020.

Research question. Do daily USD/ZAR returns show volatility clustering, and which conditional variance model (ARCH, GARCH or asymmetric GARCH, with normal or Student t errors) gives the most reliable one-day-ahead risk forecasts?

“Reliable” is made precise through Value-at-Risk (VaR). The one-day VaR at level \(p\) is the \(p\) quantile of the forecast distribution of tomorrow’s return. A good model should be exceeded on close to a fraction \(p\) of days, and those exceedances should not cluster.

4 Testing for ARCH effects

The ARCH LM test of Engle regresses the squared demeaned returns on \(q\) of their own lags,

\[ e_t^2 = a_0 + a_1 e_{t-1}^2 + \dots + a_q e_{t-q}^2 + u_t , \]

and uses \(LM = T R^2 \sim \chi^2_q\) under the null hypothesis of no ARCH effects. I implemented the test directly rather than using a package, so that each step is visible.

Code 
arch_lm <- function(e, q = 12) {
  e2 <- e^2
  n  <- length(e2)
  X  <- sapply(1:q, function(k) e2[(q - k + 1):(n - k)])   # lagged squares
  y  <- e2[(q + 1):n]
  r2 <- summary(lm(y ~ X))$r.squared
  stat <- length(y) * r2
  c(statistic = stat, df = q, p.value = pchisq(stat, q, lower.tail = FALSE))
}

arch_raw <- arch_lm(r - mean(r), q = 12)
arch_raw
   statistic           df      p.value 
1.781493e+02 1.200000e+01 1.022693e-31

With 12 lags the statistic is 178.1 on 12 degrees of freedom (p < 0.001). The test rejects the null hypothesis of a constant variance, so a model for the conditional variance is needed. Fitting a constant variance model here would produce prediction intervals that are too wide in calm periods and far too narrow in turbulent periods, which is exactly when accurate intervals matter most.

5 Models

5.1 The Box and Jenkins loop for returns

The project follows the Box and Jenkins workflow from the notes, applied to both equations of the model:

  1. Plot the data: done in the exploratory analysis.
  2. Difference until approximately stationary: working with log returns is the first difference of the log exchange rate. The returns fluctuate around a constant mean, so no further differencing is needed.
  3. Read the ACF and PACF to identify candidate orders: for the mean equation the ACF of the returns is close to zero at all lags, so a constant mean, ARMA(0,0), is used. For the variance equation the slowly decaying ACF of squared returns suggests that a low order ARCH model will not be enough and that GARCH(1,1) is the natural candidate, since it is an ARCH(\(\infty\)) model with geometrically decaying weights.
  4. Estimate by maximum likelihood: with rugarch, and with my own code for GARCH(1,1) (Section 6).
  5. Diagnose residuals against the white noise standard: the standardised residuals \(\hat z_t = \hat\varepsilon_t / \hat\sigma_t\) should show no autocorrelation (mean equation adequate) and their squares should show no autocorrelation (variance equation adequate).
  6. Forecast only if diagnostics pass; otherwise return to step 3.

Finally, a model that survives the loop must still beat the benchmarks out of sample (Section 7).

5.2 Candidate models

All models have a constant mean, \(r_t = \mu + \varepsilon_t\) with \(\varepsilon_t = \sigma_t z_t\), and differ in the variance equation and in the distribution of \(z_t\):

The GJR model allows good and bad news to affect volatility differently. For a share price the usual finding is that falls (negative returns) raise volatility more than rises. For USD/ZAR the interpretation is reversed: a negative return is a strengthening of the rand, so \(\gamma > 0\) would mean that rand appreciation raises volatility more, and \(\gamma < 0\) that rand depreciation raises volatility more.

Interpreting the GARCH(1,1) t fit. The ARCH coefficient \(\hat\alpha\) = 0.045 measures how strongly volatility reacts to yesterday’s shock, and \(\hat\beta\) = 0.942 measures how much of yesterday’s volatility carries over. Their sum, the persistence, is 0.9874, very close to 1. A volatility shock therefore dies out slowly, with a half-life of about 55 trading days. The implied long-run (unconditional) standard deviation is 0.945% a day, or 15.0% a year, and forecasts of volatility revert towards this level.

Normal or Student t errors? Even after allowing for a changing variance, the estimated degrees of freedom are \(\hat\nu\) = 11.0, far from the normal limit (\(\nu \to \infty\)). The log likelihood improves by 34.1 when moving from normal to t errors for the same variance equation, for just one extra parameter, so the standardised returns are still fat-tailed. This matters for risk: a normal model will understate the probability of extreme days.

Asymmetry. The GJR term is \(\hat\gamma\) = -0.051 (p < 0.001). It is negative and significant, so shocks that weaken the rand (positive returns) raise volatility more than shocks that strengthen it. This is the reverse of the leverage effect in share prices, and is typical of emerging market currencies, where sudden depreciations are associated with stress and capital outflows. By BIC the preferred model is the GJR-GARCH(1,1) t model.

6 My own implementation of GARCH(1,1)

To make sure I understand what rugarch is doing, I wrote the GARCH(1,1) likelihood myself. With Gaussian errors the conditional density of \(r_t\) given the past is \(N(\mu, \sigma_t^2)\), so by the prediction error decomposition the log likelihood is

\[ \ell(\mu, \omega, \alpha, \beta) = -\frac{1}{2} \sum_{t=1}^{T} \left[ \log(2\pi) + \log \sigma_t^2 + \frac{(r_t - \mu)^2}{\sigma_t^2} \right], \qquad \sigma_t^2 = \omega + \alpha (r_{t-1} - \mu)^2 + \beta \sigma_{t-1}^2 . \]

The recursion is started at \(\sigma_1^2 = \frac{1}{T}\sum (r_t - \mu)^2\). The constraints \(\omega > 0\), \(\alpha, \beta \ge 0\) and \(\alpha + \beta < 1\) (needed for a finite unconditional variance) are imposed by optimising over unconstrained working parameters,

\[ \omega = e^{\theta_2}, \quad \alpha = \frac{e^{\theta_3}}{1 + e^{\theta_3} + e^{\theta_4}}, \quad \beta = \frac{e^{\theta_4}}{1 + e^{\theta_3} + e^{\theta_4}}, \]

Standard errors come from the inverse of the numerical Hessian of the negative log likelihood. The Hessian is computed on the working scale, because a numerical derivative on the natural scale can step outside the region \(\alpha + \beta < 1\) when the estimates are close to it (which they are for persistent series like this one), giving a negative variance and an undefined likelihood. The working scale covariance \(V_\theta\) is then mapped to the natural scale with the delta method, \(V = J V_\theta J^\top\), where \(J\) is the Jacobian of the transformation.

Code 
garch11_filter <- function(par, r) {
  mu <- par[1]; omega <- par[2]; alpha <- par[3]; beta <- par[4]
  e  <- r - mu
  s2 <- numeric(length(r))
  s2[1] <- mean(e^2)
  for (t in 2:length(r)) s2[t] <- omega + alpha * e[t - 1]^2 + beta * s2[t - 1]
  list(e = e, sigma2 = s2)
}

garch11_nll <- function(par, r) {
  f <- garch11_filter(par, r)
  # guard against invalid variances (can only happen at the very edge of
  # the parameter space during numerical differentiation)
  if (any(!is.finite(f$sigma2)) || any(f$sigma2 <= 0)) return(1e10)
  0.5 * sum(log(2 * pi) + log(f$sigma2) + f$e^2 / f$sigma2)
}

to_natural <- function(theta) {
  d <- 1 + exp(theta[3]) + exp(theta[4])
  c(mu = theta[1], omega = exp(theta[2]),
    alpha = exp(theta[3]) / d, beta = exp(theta[4]) / d)
}

to_working <- function(par) {
  rest <- 1 - par[3] - par[4]
  c(par[1], log(par[2]), log(par[3] / rest), log(par[4] / rest))
}

# numerical Jacobian by central differences, used for the delta method
jacobian_num <- function(f, x, h = 1e-6) {
  J <- matrix(0, length(f(x)), length(x))
  for (j in seq_along(x)) {
    xp <- x; xm <- x
    xp[j] <- x[j] + h; xm[j] <- x[j] - h
    J[, j] <- (f(xp) - f(xm)) / (2 * h)
  }
  J
}

fit_garch11 <- function(r, start = c(mean(r), 0.1 * var(r), 0.05, 0.90), se = TRUE) {
  nll_w <- function(th) garch11_nll(to_natural(th), r)
  opt <- optim(to_working(start), nll_w, method = "BFGS",
               control = list(maxit = 2000, reltol = 1e-10))
  est <- to_natural(opt$par)

  se_nat <- rep(NA_real_, 4)
  if (se) {
    # Hessian on the unconstrained working scale, then the delta method:
    # Var(natural) = J Var(working) J', with J the Jacobian of to_natural()
    H_w <- optimHess(opt$par, nll_w)
    V_w <- tryCatch(solve(H_w), error = function(e) matrix(NA_real_, 4, 4))
    J   <- jacobian_num(to_natural, opt$par)
    se_nat <- sqrt(pmax(diag(J %*% V_w %*% t(J)), 0))
  }

  list(estimate = est,
       se = setNames(se_nat, names(est)),
       loglik = -opt$value,
       convergence = opt$convergence,
       sigma2 = garch11_filter(est, r)$sigma2)
}

own <- fit_garch11(r)

7 Out-of-sample evaluation: Value-at-Risk backtest

7.1 Design

In-sample fit does not answer the research question, which is about forecasts. The last 1000 trading days (from 30 September 2022 to 30 September 2026) are used as a test period. For each day in the test period, every model produces a one-day-ahead forecast of the mean and standard deviation using only data up to the previous day. The GARCH models are re-estimated every 25 trading days on an expanding window, which keeps the evaluation free of temporal leakage.

From each forecast distribution I compute the VaR at four levels: the 1% and 5% quantiles (losses for someone holding dollars when the rand strengthens) and the 95% and 99% quantiles (losses for someone who needs dollars, such as an importer, when the rand weakens). For Student t models,

\[ \text{VaR}_{p,t} = \hat\mu_{t|t-1} + \hat\sigma_{t|t-1} \, t_{\nu}^{-1}(p) \sqrt{(\nu - 2)/\nu}, \]

where the last factor rescales the t distribution to unit variance.

Two benchmarks without a GARCH variance are included, in the spirit of the benchmark methods in the notes:

  • Historical simulation (HS): the VaR is the empirical quantile of the previous 250 returns.
  • Rolling normal: a normal distribution with the mean and variance of the previous 250 returns.

The ARCH(1) model failed the residual diagnostics, so following step 6 it is not part of the forecast comparison.

7.2 Backtest tests

For each model and level, a hit is a day on which the realised return falls beyond the VaR (below it for the 1% and 5% levels, above it for the 95% and 99% levels). Under a correct model the hits are independent Bernoulli(\(p^*\)) variables, where \(p^* = p\) for the lower tail and \(p^* = 1 - p\) for the upper tail. I implemented two standard likelihood ratio tests.

  • Kupiec unconditional coverage test: is the hit rate equal to \(p^*\)? With \(x\) hits in \(T\) days and \(\hat\pi = x / T\), \(LR_{uc} = -2 \left[ (T - x)\log(1 - p^*) + x \log p^* - (T - x)\log(1 - \hat\pi) - x \log \hat\pi \right] \sim \chi^2_1 .\)
  • Christoffersen conditional coverage test: adds a test of independence, comparing a first-order Markov chain for the hits with an independent sequence, so that clustered hits are detected. \(LR_{cc} = LR_{uc} + LR_{ind} \sim \chi^2_2\).
Code 
kupiec_test <- function(hits, p) {
  n <- length(hits); x <- sum(hits); phat <- x / n
  ll0 <- (n - x) * log(1 - p) + x * log(p)
  ll1 <- if (x == 0 || x == n) 0 else (n - x) * log(1 - phat) + x * log(phat)
  lr  <- -2 * (ll0 - ll1)
  c(hits = x, expected = n * p, LR_uc = lr, p_uc = pchisq(lr, 1, lower.tail = FALSE))
}

christoffersen_test <- function(hits, p) {
  h0 <- hits[-length(hits)]; h1 <- hits[-1]
  n00 <- sum(h0 == 0 & h1 == 0); n01 <- sum(h0 == 0 & h1 == 1)
  n10 <- sum(h0 == 1 & h1 == 0); n11 <- sum(h0 == 1 & h1 == 1)
  pi01 <- n01 / (n00 + n01)
  pi11 <- if ((n10 + n11) > 0) n11 / (n10 + n11) else 0
  pi1  <- (n01 + n11) / (n00 + n01 + n10 + n11)
  xlogy <- function(x, y) ifelse(x == 0, 0, x * log(y))
  ll_ind <- xlogy(n00, 1 - pi01) + xlogy(n01, pi01) +
            xlogy(n10, 1 - pi11) + xlogy(n11, pi11)
  ll_dep <- xlogy(n00 + n10, 1 - pi1) + xlogy(n01 + n11, pi1)
  lr_ind <- -2 * (ll_dep - ll_ind)
  lr_cc  <- kupiec_test(hits, p)[["LR_uc"]] + lr_ind
  c(p_ind = pchisq(lr_ind, 1, lower.tail = FALSE),
    p_cc  = pchisq(lr_cc, 2, lower.tail = FALSE))
}

# quantile (tick) loss: the proper scoring rule for a p quantile

7.3 Variance forecast accuracy

The VaR tests look at the tails. To compare the variance forecasts as a whole I use the QLIKE loss, \(L_t = \log \hat\sigma_{t|t-1}^2 + r_t^2 / \hat\sigma_{t|t-1}^2\), with the squared return as a noisy proxy for the true variance. QLIKE is robust to that noise in the sense that it ranks forecasts correctly in expectation, and it penalises under-prediction of variance more heavily than over-prediction, which is the right asymmetry for risk management.

9 Discussion

9.1 Key findings

  1. The rand shows all the stylised facts of financial returns: almost no autocorrelation in returns, strong and slowly decaying autocorrelation in squared returns, and fat tails (excess kurtosis 1.9). The ARCH LM test confirms a time-varying variance (p < 0.001).
  2. The Box and Jenkins loop rejected ARCH(1) because ARCH structure remained in its standardised residuals, and led to GARCH(1,1).
  3. Volatility is highly persistent: \(\hat\alpha + \hat\beta\) = 0.9874, a half-life of about 55 trading days.
  4. Student t errors are clearly better than normal errors (\(\hat\nu\) = 11.0), so even after removing volatility clustering, extreme days are more common than a normal distribution implies.
  5. Asymmetry: \(\hat\gamma\) = -0.051 (p < 0.001). There is significant asymmetry in how depreciation and appreciation shocks affect volatility.
  6. Out of sample, the preferred model by quantile loss is GJR-GARCH(1,1) t and by QLIKE GJR-GARCH(1,1) t. The number of rejected VaR tests (out of four levels) was 3 for GARCH(1,1) t against 0 for historical simulation and 2 for the rolling normal benchmark.

9.2 Issues and limitations

  • Power of the tests. With 1000 test days, a correct 1% VaR is expected to be exceeded only 10 times, so the Kupiec test has little power to separate a 1% model from, say, a 1.5% model. A model that “passes” is not proven correct.
  • Tests with a large sample. The residual tests in-sample use more than 4000 observations, so even very small departures from white noise can be significant. I combined the formal tests with the plots and the out-of-sample results rather than relying on p-values alone.
  • Very high persistence. A persistence close to 1 can be a sign of structural breaks in the level of volatility (for example a calmer period after 2020) rather than true long memory, as discussed in the notes. A model with a regime change in volatility would be a natural alternative, and this is the subject of Project 4.
  • Data choices. FRED noon rates in New York miss moves that happen during South African trading hours after the New York fix, and dropping US holidays makes some returns cover two days. Neither should affect the conclusions much, but a Johannesburg close series would be a useful check.
  • Refitting every 25 days rather than daily saves time but means the parameters lag slightly behind; the volatility itself is still updated every day.
  • VaR is only one risk measure. It says nothing about how large losses are once the VaR is exceeded. Expected shortfall would be the natural extension.

9.3 What I would do differently

I would use a longer test period, add expected shortfall to the backtest, and compare GARCH with a hidden Markov model that lets volatility switch between a small number of regimes (Project 4), using the same returns and the same backtest so that the two approaches can be compared directly.

# Introduction and research question

Exchange rate returns behave very differently from the seasonal series in Project 1. The level of the rand is close to a random walk, so its changes are almost unpredictable, but the *size* of those changes is not: calm periods and turbulent periods come in clusters. ARIMA models describe the conditional mean and assume a constant variance, so they cannot capture this. ARCH and GARCH models add a second equation for the conditional variance.

The rand is a useful series for this because it is one of the most traded and most volatile emerging market currencies, and its history since 2010 includes several clear shocks, for example the dismissal of the finance minister in December 2015 and the start of the COVID-19 pandemic in March 2020.

**Research question.** *Do daily USD/ZAR returns show volatility clustering, and which conditional variance model (ARCH, GARCH or asymmetric GARCH, with normal or Student t errors) gives the most reliable one-day-ahead risk forecasts?*

"Reliable" is made precise through Value-at-Risk (VaR). The one-day VaR at level $p$ is the $p$ quantile of the forecast distribution of tomorrow's return. A good model should be exceeded on close to a fraction $p$ of days, and those exceedances should not cluster.

# Testing for ARCH effects

The ARCH LM test of Engle regresses the squared demeaned returns on $q$ of their own lags,

$$
e_t^2 = a_0 + a_1 e_{t-1}^2 + \dots + a_q e_{t-q}^2 + u_t ,
$$

and uses $LM = T R^2 \sim \chi^2_q$ under the null hypothesis of no ARCH effects. I implemented the test directly rather than using a package, so that each step is visible.

```{r}
#| label: arch-lm

arch_lm <- function(e, q = 12) {
  e2 <- e^2
  n  <- length(e2)
  X  <- sapply(1:q, function(k) e2[(q - k + 1):(n - k)])   # lagged squares
  y  <- e2[(q + 1):n]
  r2 <- summary(lm(y ~ X))$r.squared
  stat <- length(y) * r2
  c(statistic = stat, df = q, p.value = pchisq(stat, q, lower.tail = FALSE))
}

arch_raw <- arch_lm(r - mean(r), q = 12)
arch_raw
```

With 12 lags the statistic is `r fmt(arch_raw["statistic"], 1)` on 12 degrees of freedom (p `r pfmt(arch_raw["p.value"])`). The test `r rejects(arch_raw["p.value"])` the null hypothesis of a constant variance, so a model for the conditional variance is needed. Fitting a constant variance model here would produce prediction intervals that are too wide in calm periods and far too narrow in turbulent periods, which is exactly when accurate intervals matter most.

# Models

## The Box and Jenkins loop for returns

The project follows the Box and Jenkins workflow from the notes, applied to both equations of the model:

1. **Plot the data:** done in the exploratory analysis.
2. **Difference until approximately stationary:** working with log returns is the first difference of the log exchange rate. The returns fluctuate around a constant mean, so no further differencing is needed.
3. **Read the ACF and PACF to identify candidate orders:** for the *mean* equation the ACF of the returns is close to zero at all lags, so a constant mean, ARMA(0,0), is used. For the *variance* equation the slowly decaying ACF of squared returns suggests that a low order ARCH model will not be enough and that GARCH(1,1) is the natural candidate, since it is an ARCH($\infty$) model with geometrically decaying weights.
4. **Estimate by maximum likelihood:** with `rugarch`, and with my own code for GARCH(1,1) (@sec-own-code).
5. **Diagnose residuals against the white noise standard:** the standardised residuals $\hat z_t = \hat\varepsilon_t / \hat\sigma_t$ should show no autocorrelation (mean equation adequate) and their squares should show no autocorrelation (variance equation adequate).
6. **Forecast only if diagnostics pass; otherwise return to step 3.**

Finally, a model that survives the loop must still beat the benchmarks out of sample (@sec-backtest).

## Candidate models

All models have a constant mean, $r_t = \mu + \varepsilon_t$ with $\varepsilon_t = \sigma_t z_t$, and differ in the variance equation and in the distribution of $z_t$:

| Model | Variance equation | Distribution of $z_t$ |
|:--|:--|:--|
| ARCH(1) | $\sigma_t^2 = \omega + \alpha \varepsilon_{t-1}^2$ | Normal |
| GARCH(1,1) normal | $\sigma_t^2 = \omega + \alpha \varepsilon_{t-1}^2 + \beta \sigma_{t-1}^2$ | Normal |
| GARCH(1,1) t | as above | Standardised Student t with $\nu$ degrees of freedom |
| GJR-GARCH(1,1) t | $\sigma_t^2 = \omega + (\alpha + \gamma I_{t-1}) \varepsilon_{t-1}^2 + \beta \sigma_{t-1}^2$, with $I_{t-1} = 1$ if $\varepsilon_{t-1} < 0$ | Standardised Student t |

: The four candidate models. {#tbl-models}

The GJR model allows good and bad news to affect volatility differently. For a share price the usual finding is that falls (negative returns) raise volatility more than rises. For USD/ZAR the interpretation is reversed: a *negative* return is a *strengthening* of the rand, so $\gamma > 0$ would mean that rand appreciation raises volatility more, and $\gamma < 0$ that rand depreciation raises volatility more.

# My own implementation of GARCH(1,1) {#sec-own-code}

To make sure I understand what `rugarch` is doing, I wrote the GARCH(1,1) likelihood myself. With Gaussian errors the conditional density of $r_t$ given the past is $N(\mu, \sigma_t^2)$, so by the prediction error decomposition the log likelihood is

$$
\ell(\mu, \omega, \alpha, \beta) = -\frac{1}{2} \sum_{t=1}^{T} \left[ \log(2\pi) + \log \sigma_t^2 + \frac{(r_t - \mu)^2}{\sigma_t^2} \right],
\qquad
\sigma_t^2 = \omega + \alpha (r_{t-1} - \mu)^2 + \beta \sigma_{t-1}^2 .
$$

The recursion is started at $\sigma_1^2 = \frac{1}{T}\sum (r_t - \mu)^2$. The constraints $\omega > 0$, $\alpha, \beta \ge 0$ and $\alpha + \beta < 1$ (needed for a finite unconditional variance) are imposed by optimising over unconstrained *working* parameters,

$$
\omega = e^{\theta_2}, \quad \alpha = \frac{e^{\theta_3}}{1 + e^{\theta_3} + e^{\theta_4}}, \quad \beta = \frac{e^{\theta_4}}{1 + e^{\theta_3} + e^{\theta_4}},
$$

Standard errors come from the inverse of the numerical Hessian of the negative log likelihood. The Hessian is computed on the working scale, because a numerical derivative on the natural scale can step outside the region $\alpha + \beta < 1$ when the estimates are close to it (which they are for persistent series like this one), giving a negative variance and an undefined likelihood. The working scale covariance $V_\theta$ is then mapped to the natural scale with the delta method, $V = J V_\theta J^\top$, where $J$ is the Jacobian of the transformation.

```{r}
#| label: own-garch

garch11_filter <- function(par, r) {
  mu <- par[1]; omega <- par[2]; alpha <- par[3]; beta <- par[4]
  e  <- r - mu
  s2 <- numeric(length(r))
  s2[1] <- mean(e^2)
  for (t in 2:length(r)) s2[t] <- omega + alpha * e[t - 1]^2 + beta * s2[t - 1]
  list(e = e, sigma2 = s2)
}

garch11_nll <- function(par, r) {
  f <- garch11_filter(par, r)
  # guard against invalid variances (can only happen at the very edge of
  # the parameter space during numerical differentiation)
  if (any(!is.finite(f$sigma2)) || any(f$sigma2 <= 0)) return(1e10)
  0.5 * sum(log(2 * pi) + log(f$sigma2) + f$e^2 / f$sigma2)
}

to_natural <- function(theta) {
  d <- 1 + exp(theta[3]) + exp(theta[4])
  c(mu = theta[1], omega = exp(theta[2]),
    alpha = exp(theta[3]) / d, beta = exp(theta[4]) / d)
}

to_working <- function(par) {
  rest <- 1 - par[3] - par[4]
  c(par[1], log(par[2]), log(par[3] / rest), log(par[4] / rest))
}

# numerical Jacobian by central differences, used for the delta method
jacobian_num <- function(f, x, h = 1e-6) {
  J <- matrix(0, length(f(x)), length(x))
  for (j in seq_along(x)) {
    xp <- x; xm <- x
    xp[j] <- x[j] + h; xm[j] <- x[j] - h
    J[, j] <- (f(xp) - f(xm)) / (2 * h)
  }
  J
}

fit_garch11 <- function(r, start = c(mean(r), 0.1 * var(r), 0.05, 0.90), se = TRUE) {
  nll_w <- function(th) garch11_nll(to_natural(th), r)
  opt <- optim(to_working(start), nll_w, method = "BFGS",
               control = list(maxit = 2000, reltol = 1e-10))
  est <- to_natural(opt$par)

  se_nat <- rep(NA_real_, 4)
  if (se) {
    # Hessian on the unconstrained working scale, then the delta method:
    # Var(natural) = J Var(working) J', with J the Jacobian of to_natural()
    H_w <- optimHess(opt$par, nll_w)
    V_w <- tryCatch(solve(H_w), error = function(e) matrix(NA_real_, 4, 4))
    J   <- jacobian_num(to_natural, opt$par)
    se_nat <- sqrt(pmax(diag(J %*% V_w %*% t(J)), 0))
  }

  list(estimate = est,
       se = setNames(se_nat, names(est)),
       loglik = -opt$value,
       convergence = opt$convergence,
       sigma2 = garch11_filter(est, r)$sigma2)
}

own <- fit_garch11(r)
```

## A small simulation check

Agreement with a package shows that two implementations agree, not that either is right. As a second check, I simulated 100 series of the same length as the data from a GARCH(1,1) with known parameters (set equal to the `rugarch` estimates) and refitted each one with my code.

```{r}
#| label: simulation

sim_garch11 <- function(n, mu, omega, alpha, beta, burn = 500) {
  N <- n + burn
  z <- rnorm(N); e <- s2 <- numeric(N)
  s2[1] <- omega / (1 - alpha - beta)
  e[1]  <- sqrt(s2[1]) * z[1]
  for (t in 2:N) {
    s2[t] <- omega + alpha * e[t - 1]^2 + beta * s2[t - 1]
    e[t]  <- sqrt(s2[t]) * z[t]
  }
  mu + e[(burn + 1):N]
}
```

# Out-of-sample evaluation: Value-at-Risk backtest {#sec-backtest}

## Design

In-sample fit does not answer the research question, which is about forecasts. The last `r n_test` trading days (from `r format(fx$date[n - n_test + 1], "%d %B %Y")` to `r format(fx$date[n], "%d %B %Y")`) are used as a test period. For each day in the test period, every model produces a one-day-ahead forecast of the mean and standard deviation using only data up to the previous day. The GARCH models are re-estimated every `r refit_every` trading days on an expanding window, which keeps the evaluation free of temporal leakage.

From each forecast distribution I compute the VaR at four levels: the 1% and 5% quantiles (losses for someone holding dollars when the rand strengthens) and the 95% and 99% quantiles (losses for someone who needs dollars, such as an importer, when the rand weakens). For Student t models,

$$
\text{VaR}_{p,t} = \hat\mu_{t|t-1} + \hat\sigma_{t|t-1} \, t_{\nu}^{-1}(p) \sqrt{(\nu - 2)/\nu},
$$

where the last factor rescales the t distribution to unit variance.

Two benchmarks without a GARCH variance are included, in the spirit of the benchmark methods in the notes:

* **Historical simulation (HS):** the VaR is the empirical quantile of the previous `r hs_window` returns.
* **Rolling normal:** a normal distribution with the mean and variance of the previous `r hs_window` returns.

The ARCH(1) model failed the residual diagnostics, so following step 6 it is not part of the forecast comparison.

## Backtest tests

For each model and level, a *hit* is a day on which the realised return falls beyond the VaR (below it for the 1% and 5% levels, above it for the 95% and 99% levels). Under a correct model the hits are independent Bernoulli($p^*$) variables, where $p^* = p$ for the lower tail and $p^* = 1 - p$ for the upper tail. I implemented two standard likelihood ratio tests.

* **Kupiec unconditional coverage test:** is the hit rate equal to $p^*$? With $x$ hits in $T$ days and $\hat\pi = x / T$,
  $LR_{uc} = -2 \left[ (T - x)\log(1 - p^*) + x \log p^* - (T - x)\log(1 - \hat\pi) - x \log \hat\pi \right] \sim \chi^2_1 .$
* **Christoffersen conditional coverage test:** adds a test of independence, comparing a first-order Markov chain for the hits with an independent sequence, so that clustered hits are detected. $LR_{cc} = LR_{uc} + LR_{ind} \sim \chi^2_2$.

```{r}
#| label: backtest-functions

kupiec_test <- function(hits, p) {
  n <- length(hits); x <- sum(hits); phat <- x / n
  ll0 <- (n - x) * log(1 - p) + x * log(p)
  ll1 <- if (x == 0 || x == n) 0 else (n - x) * log(1 - phat) + x * log(phat)
  lr  <- -2 * (ll0 - ll1)
  c(hits = x, expected = n * p, LR_uc = lr, p_uc = pchisq(lr, 1, lower.tail = FALSE))
}

christoffersen_test <- function(hits, p) {
  h0 <- hits[-length(hits)]; h1 <- hits[-1]
  n00 <- sum(h0 == 0 & h1 == 0); n01 <- sum(h0 == 0 & h1 == 1)
  n10 <- sum(h0 == 1 & h1 == 0); n11 <- sum(h0 == 1 & h1 == 1)
  pi01 <- n01 / (n00 + n01)
  pi11 <- if ((n10 + n11) > 0) n11 / (n10 + n11) else 0
  pi1  <- (n01 + n11) / (n00 + n01 + n10 + n11)
  xlogy <- function(x, y) ifelse(x == 0, 0, x * log(y))
  ll_ind <- xlogy(n00, 1 - pi01) + xlogy(n01, pi01) +
            xlogy(n10, 1 - pi11) + xlogy(n11, pi11)
  ll_dep <- xlogy(n00 + n10, 1 - pi1) + xlogy(n01 + n11, pi1)
  lr_ind <- -2 * (ll_dep - ll_ind)
  lr_cc  <- kupiec_test(hits, p)[["LR_uc"]] + lr_ind
  c(p_ind = pchisq(lr_ind, 1, lower.tail = FALSE),
    p_cc  = pchisq(lr_cc, 2, lower.tail = FALSE))
}

# quantile (tick) loss: the proper scoring rule for a p quantile
tick_loss <- function(y, q, p) mean((p - (y < q)) * (y - q))
```

## Variance forecast accuracy

The VaR tests look at the tails. To compare the variance forecasts as a whole I use the QLIKE loss, $L_t = \log \hat\sigma_{t|t-1}^2 + r_t^2 / \hat\sigma_{t|t-1}^2$, with the squared return as a noisy proxy for the true variance. QLIKE is robust to that noise in the sense that it ranks forecasts correctly in expectation, and it penalises under-prediction of variance more heavily than over-prediction, which is the right asymmetry for risk management.

# Discussion

## Key findings {#sec-findings}

1. **The rand shows all the stylised facts of financial returns:** almost no autocorrelation in returns, strong and slowly decaying autocorrelation in squared returns, and fat tails (excess kurtosis `r fmt(kurt - 3, 1)`). The ARCH LM test confirms a time-varying variance (p `r pfmt(arch_raw["p.value"])`).
2. **The Box and Jenkins loop rejected ARCH(1)** because ARCH structure remained in its standardised residuals, and led to GARCH(1,1).
3. **Volatility is highly persistent:** $\hat\alpha + \hat\beta$ = `r fmt(pers_g, 4)`, a half-life of about `r fmt(half_g, 0)` trading days.
4. **Student t errors are clearly better than normal errors** ($\hat\nu$ = `r fmt(nu_g, 1)`), so even after removing volatility clustering, extreme days are more common than a normal distribution implies.
5. **Asymmetry:** $\hat\gamma$ = `r fmt(gamma_j, 3)` (p `r pfmt(gamma_p)`). `r if (gamma_p < 0.05) "There is significant asymmetry in how depreciation and appreciation shocks affect volatility." else "There is no significant asymmetry."`
6. **Out of sample**, the preferred model by quantile loss is `r best_tick` and by QLIKE `r best_qlike`. The number of rejected VaR tests (out of four levels) was `r rej[["GARCH(1,1) t"]]` for GARCH(1,1) t against `r rej[["Historical simulation"]]` for historical simulation and `r rej[["Rolling normal"]]` for the rolling normal benchmark.

## Issues and limitations

* **Power of the tests.** With `r n_test` test days, a correct 1% VaR is expected to be exceeded only `r fmt(n_test * 0.01, 0)` times, so the Kupiec test has little power to separate a 1% model from, say, a 1.5% model. A model that "passes" is not proven correct.
* **Tests with a large sample.** The residual tests in-sample use more than 4000 observations, so even very small departures from white noise can be significant. I combined the formal tests with the plots and the out-of-sample results rather than relying on p-values alone.
* **Very high persistence.** A persistence close to 1 can be a sign of structural breaks in the level of volatility (for example a calmer period after 2020) rather than true long memory, as discussed in the notes. A model with a regime change in volatility would be a natural alternative, and this is the subject of Project 4.
* **Data choices.** FRED noon rates in New York miss moves that happen during South African trading hours after the New York fix, and dropping US holidays makes some returns cover two days. Neither should affect the conclusions much, but a Johannesburg close series would be a useful check.
* **Refitting every 25 days** rather than daily saves time but means the parameters lag slightly behind; the volatility itself is still updated every day.
* **VaR is only one risk measure.** It says nothing about how large losses are once the VaR is exceeded. Expected shortfall would be the natural extension.

## What I would do differently

I would use a longer test period, add expected shortfall to the backtest, and compare GARCH with a hidden Markov model that lets volatility switch between a small number of regimes (Project 4), using the same returns and the same backtest so that the two approaches can be compared directly.
